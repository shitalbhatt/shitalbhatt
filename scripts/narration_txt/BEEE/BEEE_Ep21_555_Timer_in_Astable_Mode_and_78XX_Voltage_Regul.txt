[0:42] SCENE 1: INSIDE THE 555 TIMER
The 555 timer gets its name from three 5 kilo-ohm resistors in a voltage divider inside it. They create two reference levels: two thirds of the supply and one third of the supply. Two comparators watch an external capacitor voltage. The threshold comparator on pin 6 senses when the voltage exceeds two thirds of V C C. The trigger comparator on pin 2 senses when it falls below one third.

The comparators set and reset a flip-flop whose output drives pin 3. A transistor connected to pin 7 discharges the capacitor when the output is low. With just two resistors and a capacitor outside, this arrangement creates a continuous square wave: the astable mode.

[1:31] SCENE 2: ASTABLE MODE: OPERATION
In astable mode, pins 2 and 6 are tied together and connected to the capacitor. When the power is switched on, the capacitor charges through R 1 plus R 2. While its voltage is below two thirds of the supply, the output is high. When it reaches two thirds, the upper comparator resets the flip-flop, the output goes low, and the discharge transistor turns on. Now the capacitor discharges through R 2 into pin 7. When its voltage falls to one third of the supply, the lower comparator sets the flip-flop, and the cycle repeats.

The high time is 0.693 times R 1 plus R 2, times C. The low time is 0.693 times R 2 times C. The total period is 0.693 times R 1 plus 2 R 2, times C. So the frequency is 1.44 divided by R 1 plus 2 R 2, times C. The duty cycle is R 1 plus R 2, divided by R 1 plus 2 R 2, always greater than 50 percent in this simple circuit. To get close to 50 percent, make R 1 much smaller than R 2.

[2:51] SCENE 3: 78XX FIXED VOLTAGE REGULATORS
The 78XX series are three-terminal fixed positive voltage regulators. The last two digits give the output voltage: 7805 for 5 volts, 7812 for 12 volts, and so on. The three pins are input, common ground, and output. Inside, there is a reference, an error amplifier, a series pass transistor, and protection against overheating and short circuits.

The input must be at least about 2 volts higher than the output, called the dropout. So a 7805 needs at least 7 volts at its input. Small capacitors, about 0.33 microfarad at the input and 0.1 at the output, keep it stable. The regulator behaves like a series pass transistor, so the extra voltage appears across it, and the power dissipated is the voltage difference times the load current. For high currents, a heat sink is essential. The complementary 79XX series gives negative voltages.

For better efficiency at large voltage differences, switching regulators are used. But for low-noise, low-cost designs, the 78XX linear regulator is still very popular.

[4:02] SOLVED NUMERICAL
Part A. A 555 astable circuit has R 1 equal to 1 kilo-ohm, R 2 equal to 10 kilo-ohm, and C equal to 0.1 microfarad. Find the output frequency, the high and low times, and the duty cycle. Part B. A 7805 regulator is fed from 12 volts and drives a 100 ohm load. Find the load current and the power dissipated in the regulator.

Part A, step one: R 1 plus 2 R 2 equals 1 plus 20, equals 21 kilo-ohm. The period T equals 0.693 into 21 kilo-ohm into 0.1 microfarad. That is 0.693 into 2.1 milliseconds, equals 1.455 milliseconds. So the frequency is about 687 hertz. The formula 1.44 over 21 kilo-ohm times 0.1 microfarad gives 686 hertz, which is the same within rounding.

Step two: the high time is 0.693 into 11 kilo-ohm into 0.1 microfarad, equals 0.762 milliseconds. The low time is 0.693 into 10 kilo-ohm into 0.1 microfarad, equals 0.693 milliseconds. Together, 1.455 milliseconds.

Step three: the duty cycle is 0.762 divided by 1.455, which is 52.4 percent. This agrees with R 1 plus R 2 over R 1 plus 2 R 2, which is 11 over 21.

Part B. The output is 5 volts, so the load current is 5 by 100, equals 50 milliamperes. The regulator drops 12 minus 5, equals 7 volts, so it dissipates 7 into 0.05, equals 0.35 watt. This is small, and the regulator stays cool. But for a load current of 500 milliamperes, the dissipation would be 3.5 watts, and a heat sink would be essential. That is the price of a linear regulator: the extra voltage turns directly into heat.

Both of these chips are cheap and simple, which is why they have been used for decades.

[6:07] COMMON MISTAKES AND EXAM TIPS
Four points. One: the 0.693 factor is the natural logarithm of 2, from charging between one third and two thirds of the supply. Two: in the basic astable circuit, the duty cycle is always above 50 percent, because the capacitor charges through R 1 plus R 2 but discharges only through R 2. Three: check the dropout. A 7805 needs at least 7 volts at the input, or the output will fall below 5 volts.

Four: always add the recommended capacitors close to the regulator pins, otherwise it may oscillate. And check the power dissipation: a regulator that runs too hot will shut down, thanks to its thermal protection.

Real-life use: the 555 generates clocks, flashes LEDs, drives buzzers and makes simple tone generators. The 78XX family provides the stable supply for countless microcontroller boards.
