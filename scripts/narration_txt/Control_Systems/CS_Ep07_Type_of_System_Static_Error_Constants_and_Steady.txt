[0:37] SCENE 1: ERROR AND THE FINAL VALUE THEOREM
Let us start with the definition. The error is the difference between the desired output and the actual output. For a unity feedback system, E of s equals R of s divided by 1 plus G of s. The steady-state error is what remains as time tends to infinity. Using the final value theorem, e steady state equals the limit, as s tends to zero, of s times E of s.

One very important condition. The final value theorem works only if the closed-loop system is stable. If the system is unstable, the error grows without limit, and a formula answer would be meaningless. So in the exam, check stability first, preferably with the Routh criterion.

[1:27] SCENE 2: TYPE OF SYSTEM AND ERROR CONSTANTS
The type of a system is the number of poles of the open-loop transfer function G of s into H of s at the origin. If there is no pole at the origin, it is a type 0 system. One pole at the origin: type 1. Two poles: type 2. Note that the type is determined from the open-loop function, not the closed-loop.

Now the static error constants. The position error constant Kp is the limit, as s tends to zero, of G H. The velocity error constant Kv is the limit of s G H. And the acceleration error constant Ka is the limit of s squared G H.

For type 0: Kp is a finite constant, Kv is zero, and Ka is zero. For type 1: Kp is infinite, Kv is finite, Ka is zero. For type 2: Kp and Kv are infinite, and Ka is finite. Each extra integrator improves the accuracy by one level.

[2:34] SCENE 3: STEADY-STATE ERROR FOR STEP, RAMP AND PARABOLIC INPUTS
Using the final value theorem, the steady-state errors are as follows. For a step of amplitude A: A by 1 plus Kp. For a ramp A t: A by Kv. For a parabolic input A t squared by 2: A by Ka.

Putting everything in a table: a type 0 system follows a step with a finite error, but cannot follow a ramp, and its error for a ramp is infinite. A type 1 system follows a step with zero error, a ramp with a finite error, and cannot follow a parabola. A type 2 system follows a step and a ramp with zero error, and a parabola with a finite error.

So if you must track a moving target with zero error, you need more integrators. But more integrators reduce stability, so the design is always a compromise.

A useful way to remember: every time you go from type 0 to type 1 to type 2, one entry in the table changes from infinity to a finite value, and one from a finite value to zero.

[3:50] SCENE 4: GENERALIZED ERROR SERIES
Static error constants work only for step, ramp and parabola. For any other input, we use the generalized error series. Expand E of s by R of s as a power series in s: C0 plus C1 s plus C2 by factorial 2 into s squared, and so on. Then the steady-state error is C0 times r of t, plus C1 times the first derivative of r, plus C2 by 2 factorial times the second derivative, and so on.

Example: G of s equals 10 by s into s plus 2. Then E by R equals s into s plus 2, divided by s squared plus 2 s plus 10. Long division gives 0.2 s plus 0.06 s squared and further terms. So C0 equals 0, C1 equals 0.2, and C2 equals 0.12. For a ramp r of t equals t, the steady-state error is C1 into 1, which is 0.2. It matches 1 by Kv, since Kv equals 10 by 2, equals 5.

[5:00] SOLVED NUMERICAL
Time for a numerical with two systems, both with unity feedback. System A has G of s equal to 10 into s plus 3, divided by s into s plus 2 into s plus 5. The input is 4 plus 6 t. System B has G of s equal to 20 by s plus 2 into s plus 5, with a step input of amplitude 6. Find the steady-state error in each case.

System A. Step one: the type. There is one s in the denominator, so it is type 1. Step two: always check stability. The characteristic equation is s into s plus 2 into s plus 5, plus 10 into s plus 3. That expands to s cubed plus 7 s squared plus 20 s plus 30. By the Routh test, 7 into 20 is 140, which is greater than 30, so the system is stable.

Step three: the constants. Kp is infinite, since there is an integrator. Kv equals the limit of s G of s, which is 10 into 3 divided by 2 into 5, equals 3. Ka is zero.

Step four: the error. For the constant part, 4, the error is 4 by 1 plus infinity, which is zero. For the ramp part, 6 t, the error is 6 by Kv, equals 6 by 3, equals 2. Total steady-state error is 2.

System B. It has no pole at the origin, so it is type 0. The closed loop denominator is s squared plus 7 s plus 10 plus 20, equals s squared plus 7 s plus 30, which is stable. Kp equals G of zero equals 20 by 10, equals 2. For a step of amplitude 6, the steady-state error is 6 by 1 plus 2, equals 2.

Both give an error of 2, but for very different reasons. System A cannot get ahead of the ramp. System B cannot reach the full value of the step. To make System B exact, we would add an integrator, which is the idea behind PI control in Unit 5.

[7:27] COMMON MISTAKES AND EXAM TIPS
Four traps to avoid. One: the type is always counted from the open-loop transfer function. Do not count poles at the origin in the closed-loop transfer function. Two: if feedback is not unity, include H of s in the constants. K p is the limit of G H, not just G.

Three: check stability before the final value theorem. An unstable system has no steady state. Four: when the input is a sum, such as 4 plus 6 t, calculate the error for each part separately and then add them. The principle of superposition applies because the system is linear.

And one practical tip: when Ka or Kv is large, the error is small. So the designer increases the gain K. But a larger K reduces damping and stability. This trade-off between accuracy and stability is at the heart of control design, and we will study it next with root locus and Bode plots.
