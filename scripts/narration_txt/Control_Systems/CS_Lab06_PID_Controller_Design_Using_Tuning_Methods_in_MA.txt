[2:41] LIVE DEMO: WORKED EXAMPLE AND EXPECTED RESULTS
Run the script. The Ziegler-Nichols controller gives a fast response with a large overshoot of about 62 percent, and a long settling time of about 10 seconds. That is typical: Ziegler-Nichols tuning is aggressive, and meant as a starting point.

Now the tuned controller. We reduced Kp from 18 to 10, increased the integral time from 1.4 to 3 seconds, so there is less integral action, and increased the derivative time to 1 second for extra damping. The result: overshoot falls to about 13 percent, and the settling time drops to about 7 seconds. The rise time is a little longer, 0.72 seconds instead of 0.58, which is the price of the smoother response.

For reference, a P-only controller with a gain of 15 overshoots by 64 percent and takes 18 seconds to settle, so adding I and D action clearly helps. Note that the plant already has an integrator, so the steady-state error to a step is zero anyway. The integral term here improves the error to a ramp.

In the lab, try changing one parameter at a time. Increase Kp: faster, but more overshoot. Increase Td: more damping. Decrease Ti: more integral action, which usually means more overshoot. Record the stepinfo values in a table.

[4:42] VIVA QUESTIONS AND MODEL ANSWERS
Before you go to the viva, test yourself. Pause after each question and answer it aloud.

Question 1. What is the purpose of the integral term? To eliminate the steady-state error.

Question 2. What is the purpose of the derivative term? To add damping, reduce overshoot and improve the settling time.

Question 3. Why is the Ziegler-Nichols response so oscillatory? Because the rules are designed for fast disturbance rejection, giving a quarter-amplitude decay, and the settings are aggressive for set-point changes.

Question 4. How do you retune the controller? Reduce Kp, increase the integral time, and increase the derivative time, changing one parameter at a time.

Question 5. Which commands did you use? tf, pid, feedback, step, stepinfo and pidtune.

[5:33] COMMON LAB MISTAKES AND VIVA TIPS
Four tips. One: the pid command takes Kp, Ki, Kd in this order, and Ki and Kd are parallel gains, not time constants. Two: convert Ti and Td correctly: Ki is Kp by Ti, and Kd is Kp times Td. Three: when tuning, change one parameter at a time, and record the effect. If you change three together, you cannot learn which one did what.

Four: use the same time axis and the same final time for all plots, so that the comparison is fair. In the viva, expect: why does ZN overshoot so much? What does the derivative term do? Why is the derivative filtered in practice? You can answer them all.

Two more tips for this experiment. First, Simulink has a PID Controller block. Double-click it, and the Tune button opens an interactive tuner with sliders for response time and transient behaviour. It is a quick way to see the trade-off, but always note down the final Kp, Ki and Kd values in your record, so that you can reproduce the result. Second, save your script. A short script that sets the gains, closes the loop and prints stepinfo lets you try ten sets of gains in a few minutes, and you can paste the table straight into your observation sheet.

When you compare tuning methods, define the criteria first: for example, overshoot below 15 percent and settling time below 8 seconds. Then you can say clearly which method meets the specification and which does not. In our example, the Ziegler-Nichols settings fail on overshoot, while the manually tuned controller meets both.
