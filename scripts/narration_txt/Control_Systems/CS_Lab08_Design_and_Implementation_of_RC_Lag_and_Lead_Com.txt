[0:34] SCENE 1: THE TWO DESIGNS
These are the two networks designed in the compensator lecture. The lag network uses R1 equal to 90 kilo-ohm, R2 equal to 10 kilo-ohm, and C equal to 100 microfarad. Its transfer function is one plus s, divided by one plus 10 s. The lead network uses R1 equal to 4.83 kilo-ohm, R2 equal to 1 kilo-ohm, and C equal to 50 microfarad. Its transfer function is 0.1716 times one plus 0.2414 s, divided by one plus 0.0414 s.

For the lag network, R1 is in series, and R2 with C forms the branch to ground. For the lead network, R1 is in parallel with C, in series with the signal, and R2 goes to ground. Use 1 percent resistors and a capacitor with low tolerance. If the exact values are not available, use the nearest standard values and recalculate the transfer function.

[1:36] SCENE 2: MEASUREMENT PROCEDURE
Connect the function generator to the input, and the oscilloscope across the input and output. Apply a sine wave of about 1 volt peak-to-peak, with no DC offset. At each frequency, measure the output and input amplitudes: the ratio gives the gain, and 20 log of the ratio gives decibels. Measure the time shift between zero crossings, delta t: the phase is 360 times the frequency times delta t. The output lags for the lag network and leads for the lead network.

Remember to convert from angular frequency to hertz: f equals omega by 2 pi. The lag network works at very low frequencies, a fraction of a hertz, so use a low-frequency generator or take fewer points. The lead network, with its 10 radians per second centre, is easy to measure at about 1.6 hertz.

[2:34] SCENE 3: VERIFYING WITH MATLAB
In MATLAB, create the two transfer functions with tf. Plot them together with bode. To get numbers at your measurement frequencies, call bode with a frequency vector, and convert the magnitude to decibels with 20 log 10. Compare these numbers with your oscilloscope readings in the record.

[3:41] LIVE DEMO: WORKED EXAMPLE AND EXPECTED READINGS
Here are the values you should expect. For the lag network: at 0.1 radian per second, the gain is minus 3 decibels and the phase minus 39 degrees. At 0.316, the centre, minus 10 decibels and the maximum lag of minus 54.9 degrees. At 1 radian per second, minus 17 decibels and minus 39 degrees. At 10 and above, the gain flattens at minus 20 decibels, which is the factor 0.1, and the phase returns toward zero.

For the lead network: the gain at low frequency is minus 15.3 decibels, that is 0.1716, the 1 by alpha attenuation. As frequency rises, the gain rises. At the centre frequency of 10 radians per second, the gain is minus 7.66 decibels, and the phase reaches the maximum lead of 45 degrees. At 100 radians per second, the gain is almost back to 0 decibels, and the phase has fallen to 11 degrees.

Real components have tolerances, so expect your readings within about 10 percent. Differences larger than that suggest a wiring mistake, a wrong component, or a loading effect from the oscilloscope. In your record, plot the measured points on the same axes as the theoretical curves and comment on any difference.

Finally, think about where these are used. A lead network in a servo loop adds phase margin and speeds up the response. A lag network lets you raise the low-frequency gain, and improves the steady-state accuracy. Together, they appear in the lag-lead compensator, which gives both improvements.

[5:59] COMMON LAB MISTAKES AND VIVA TIPS
Final practical tips. One: measure the components with a meter before building. A capacitor marked 100 microfarad may be 20 percent off. Two: connect the oscilloscope grounds and the generator ground together, otherwise you will get noise and wrong phase readings.

Three: keep the input amplitude small so the circuit stays linear, and avoid DC offset. Four: measure at the specified frequencies, and keep a neat table. In the viva you may hear: why does the lead network need an amplifier? Why is the lag network used for accuracy? You are ready to answer.
