[0:33] SCENE 1: ELEMENTS OF ROTATIONAL SYSTEMS
Rotational systems follow the same ideas as translational ones, only the words change. Force becomes torque, displacement becomes angle theta, and mass becomes the moment of inertia J. There are three elements. The inertia produces a torque J into d squared theta by dt squared. The rotary damper produces a torque B into d theta by dt, proportional to angular velocity. The torsional spring produces a torque K into theta, proportional to the twist.

Units to remember: J in kilogram metre squared, B in newton metre second per radian, and K in newton metre per radian.

[1:14] SCENE 2: TRANSFER FUNCTION OF A ROTATIONAL SYSTEM
Take a disc of inertia J, connected to a fixed wall through a torsional spring K and a damper B. An external torque T of t is applied, and the disc turns by theta of t. Apply Newton's law for rotation: the applied torque equals the sum of the opposing torques. J d2 theta by dt2, plus B d theta by dt, plus K theta, equals T of t.

Take the Laplace transform with zero initial conditions. J s squared plus B s plus K, multiplied by theta of s, equals T of s. So G of s, equal to theta of s by T of s, is one by J s squared plus B s plus K. It has exactly the same form as the translational system, which is why the analogy is so powerful.

[2:12] SCENE 3: GEAR TRAINS: REFLECTING INERTIA
Real systems have gears. Let the motor gear have N1 teeth and the load gear N2 teeth. The angles are in the inverse ratio of the teeth: theta 2 by theta 1 equals N1 by N2. The torques are in the opposite ratio: T2 by T1 equals N2 by N1. The gear train transmits power, so torque times speed stays the same, ignoring losses.

To refer the load to the motor shaft, multiply the load inertia and the load friction by the square of the ratio N1 by N2. So the equivalent inertia is J1 plus N1 by N2 whole squared into J2. This is the trick from the hook: when N2 is much larger than N1, the heavy load looks tiny at the motor shaft.

[3:06] SCENE 4: TORQUE-VOLTAGE ANALOGY
Just as before, the analogy is direct. In the torque-voltage analogy, torque is voltage, inertia J is inductance L, the damper B is resistance R, the spring K is one by capacitance, the angle theta is charge, and the angular velocity omega is current. In the torque-current analogy, torque is current, J is capacitance, B is conductance, K is one by L, and angular velocity is node voltage.

Whichever system is easier for you to build or simulate, you can use. This is how engineers test mechanical designs with electrical circuits.

[6:01] COMMON MISTAKES AND EXAM TIPS
Four points that save marks. One: the inertia is reflected with the square of the gear ratio, not the ratio itself. Students often forget the square. Two: check the direction of the ratio. When referring to the motor shaft, use N1 by N2, motor teeth over load teeth. When referring to the load shaft, you use the inverse.

Three: always work in radians and radians per second. If the speed is given in rpm, multiply by two pi and divide by sixty. Four: stick to one analogy through the whole problem. Do not mix torque-voltage elements with torque-current elements in the same circuit, or the final network will not be equivalent.

A real-life reminder: robot joints, lift motors, and washing machine drums all use gear trains. Designers choose the gear ratio to make the load look light to the motor, and this single formula is the reason.
