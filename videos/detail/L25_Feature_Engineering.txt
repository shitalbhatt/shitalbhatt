#0
Let me tell you why features matter so much. A model can only learn from the columns you give it. In many real projects, improving the features gives a larger gain than switching to a more advanced algorithm. Think of a cook: a better recipe with the same ingredients often beats an expensive new oven. In these two lectures we learn how to build good features, and then how to keep only the useful ones. This video is the first part, on building features.
#1
Let me explain the example dataset. Each row is one visit to an online shop. The columns describe how many pages the visitor saw, how long they stayed, how likely they were to leave, whether it was a returning visitor, and the month. The target, Revenue, says whether the visit ended in a purchase. Only about fifteen percent of visits buy, so this is an imbalanced problem, and we use the ROC AUC score, where zero point five means guessing. Keep this dataset in mind, because every example today comes from it.
#2
Let me explain why this result is a real shock to many people. The model is the same logistic regression throughout, and the data rows are the same. What we change is which columns are given to it. A gain of five hundredths in AUC is very large for this kind of problem, and it came from some simple additions, such as totals, ratios and flags. This is why experienced practitioners say that features matter more than algorithms. It also tells you where to spend your time in a project.
#3
Let me explain why the constructed features help. A linear model works with a weighted sum of the columns. It cannot, by itself, compute a ratio or a total, so if the useful signal is time per page, it has no way to find that from separate duration and page columns. Handing it the ratio directly makes the signal available. Domain knowledge decides which features to build. For a shopping site, you ask what a sales person would look at: effort, interest, and how much of the visit was about products.
#4
Let me give a small example of each recipe. Total: adding administrative, informational and product pages gives the total pages. Ratio: dividing duration by pages gives time per page, which removes the effect of simply visiting many pages. Flag: page value greater than zero becomes one or zero, which turns a skewed numeric column into a clean yes or no. Log: log of one plus the value reduces the long tail. Band: pandas qcut with four groups gives short, medium, long and very long visits. Pick the recipe that matches the shape of the problem.
#5
Let me explain why such a simple flag is so strong. Page value is the average value of the pages a visitor saw before reaching a purchase or leaving. A visitor who saw a page with a nonzero value is already close to buying. Most visitors have zero, and a few have large values. The flag separates the two groups cleanly, with fifty-six percent buying in one group and under four percent in the other. In pandas this is one short line, df of page values greater than zero, then astype int.
#6
Let me explain how to interpret this. With four equal-sized groups, called quartile bands, we count the purchase rate in each. It starts near four percent for the shortest visits, rises to twenty-four percent, and then stays about the same. A linear model with the raw duration assumes that every extra minute adds the same amount, which is wrong here. With bands, the model can give each band its own effect, and so it captures the flattening. This is a simple way to let a linear model handle non-linear patterns.
#7
Let me explain each practice with a short example. Domain knowledge: ask the business team what they believe matters. Never use the target: if you create a feature such as average revenue per customer using the whole table, it includes the answer, and the model looks brilliant in your notebook but fails in real life. Guard divisions: if a session has zero pages, dividing duration by zero gives infinity, so we replace zero with missing and then fill with zero. Test each feature: add one at a time, and keep it only if the score improves on the test set.
#8
Let me explain the formula in words. A model with two features, A and B, predicts a baseline plus a weight times A plus another weight times B. Adding the product A times B as a third feature lets the effect of A change with B. A simple example: advertising and festive season. Advertising helps sales a little in a normal month, and a lot in a festive month. Without an interaction term, the model has to use one single effect of advertising for every month, which is not right.
#9
Let me explain how to read this table. We compare the purchase rate for visitors with a page value flag of zero and one, separately for new and returning visitors. The jump for new visitors is from seven to eighty-six percent, which is huge, while for returning visitors it is from three to fifty-one. The jumps are not the same, so the effect of page value depends on visitor type. If the lines were parallel, there would be no interaction. This kind of two-way table is the quickest way to spot a candidate, and then you add the product as a feature and test it.
#10
Let me explain in code. From scikit-learn, PolynomialFeatures with degree equal to two and include bias equal to False creates all the squares and cross-products. It is useful to set interaction only equal to True when you want only the products and no squares. After fitting, get feature names out shows which columns were created. Use it on a small number of numeric columns that you have reason to believe interact. Applying it to everything is not a good idea, as the next slide shows.
#11
Let me explain the numbers. The formula says that with n inputs and degree d, you get n plus d choose d, minus one columns. For ten inputs at degree two, that is sixty-five columns. At degree three it is two hundred eighty-five. For our eighty-five encoded features, degree three gives more than one hundred nine thousand columns, far more than the twelve thousand rows of data. A model with more columns than rows can fit the training data perfectly even if the labels are random, which is over-fitting in its purest form.
#12
Let me explain the three safeguards. Scaling first matters because the square of a large number is a very large number, so the polynomial terms would dominate everything. Low degree matters because each extra degree adds many columns and little signal. And selection matters because once you have expanded, most of the new columns are useless, so you use an L one penalty, or a feature selection step, to keep only the helpful ones. Putting all of this in a pipeline means the steps are fitted on the training data only, which avoids leakage.
#13
Let me explain this analogy. Searching for a friend along a hundred-metre road is easy, because there is one direction to search. In a field, you have two directions. In a building with floors, three directions. Each additional feature adds a new direction, and the amount of space to search grows enormously. With four bins per feature, the number of cells is four to the power of the number of features. With ten features that is over a million cells, and our twelve thousand sessions can fill only a tiny fraction of them.
#14
Let me explain each symptom. Sparse data means that for most new points, there are no similar training points nearby, so predictions are guesses. Distance-based methods like K nearest neighbours, K-means and DBSCAN rely on the contrast between near and far, which disappears. And over-fitting is the symptom you can measure directly: train score high, test score low. If you see a large gap between them after adding many features, the model has memorised the noise, and you should remove columns or use regularisation.
#15
Let me explain how to read this chart. The horizontal axis is the number of columns, from ten up to two hundred eighty-five, and there are two lines, train and test. The train line keeps going up to almost one, and the test line falls from point eight seven eight to point seven four three. The widening gap between them is the signature of over-fitting. This experiment is easy to repeat on your own data, and it is a convincing figure for any report on why selecting features matters.
#16
Let me explain the rule of thumb. For a classifier, you want roughly ten cases of the rarer class for each feature. In the Pima diabetes data, the rarer class has two hundred sixty-eight patients, so about twenty-seven features at most. It is not a law, just a sanity check.
