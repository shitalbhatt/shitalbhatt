#0
Let me tell you why outliers deserve two full lectures. They are the points that grab attention in a plot, and they are also the points that can quietly ruin a mean, a regression line or a clustering. At the same time, an outlier is sometimes the most valuable row in the data, such as a fraud case or a rare disease. So the skill we want is not how to delete outliers, but how to decide what each one is, and then handle it in a way you can defend.
#1
Let me explain how the session is organised. First we define the idea and see why outliers appear, so that you can recognise the cause. Then the four types, because the type decides which tool can find it. Then the tools, from the simplest chart to machine learning models. Then the damage that outliers cause if ignored, and finally the nine ways to treat them. The practical session applies every step to the Airbnb data. Please keep the earlier lecture on robust statistics in mind, because the median and interquartile range come back here.
#2
Let me work through that example. The seven salaries and the director's five hundred thousand give a mean of about ninety five thousand, even though no staff member earns anything near that. The median stays at thirty nine thousand, because adding one extreme value changes the middle position by only one step. This is the breakdown point idea from lecture two. If you ever see a mean that is far from the median, treat it as an alarm, and look at the extremes before you do anything else.
#3
Let me explain what this shows. When the maximum is nearly a hundred times the median, a handful of rows are doing something unusual. Price has a typical value of about one hundred six dollars, but the largest is ten thousand. That does not tell us whether the ten thousand is a typo or a palace. The numbers only tell us that something is there, and the next slide helps us decide what. A very useful habit is to print the top ten and bottom ten values of each numeric column, and read them with your own eyes.
#4
Let me add a note to each cause. Eleven zero-dollar prices and fourteen listings asking for more than a year are almost certainly entry errors, because nobody rents a home for free for a year. The two hundred thirty nine listings above a thousand dollars are real luxury homes, so we should keep them. The five hundred thousand and one in California housing is a cap, an artefact of how the data was stored. The host with three hundred twenty seven homes is a different kind of seller. Each cause calls for a different treatment, which is why we ask why before we ask how.
#5
Let me give an example for each type. Global: a price of ten thousand dollars, far from all others. Contextual: two hundred fifty dollars is a normal price for an entire home but a strange one for a shared room, so context matters, and the same value is an outlier in one group and normal in another. Collective: a single host posting forty eight rooms at exactly the same price, which is a pattern in a group of rows, even though each row looks fine. Multivariate: eleven reviews a month is plausible, and fifteen total reviews is plausible, but together they imply the listing existed for just over a month and received those reviews, which is odd.
#6
Let me explain how to do this in pandas. Use df dot groupby of the room type, then the price column, and compute Q one, Q three and the fences per group. Then compare each row with the fence of its own group, using transform. A shared room at two hundred fifty dollars is above its group fence of one thirty eight, so it is flagged, whereas the whole-column fence of three thirty four would let it pass. Whenever your data has natural groups, such as region, product type or age band, compute outlier limits per group.
#7
Let me say a little about each. Charts are the fastest, and they show the whole picture, including whether a cluster of points forms a second group. Rules are simple, transparent and easy to explain to others, which is why they are used in most assignments. Models can find strange combinations, which no single column reveals, but they are harder to explain, and need settings that you choose. A good order of work is chart, rule, model. And whichever you use, finish with a manual look at the flagged rows.
#8
Let me say how to read it in detail. Compare where the median line sits inside the box. If it is near the bottom, the data is right-skewed. Then look at the whiskers, which stop at the last point inside the fences, not at the maximum. Count the dots to get a rough idea of the number of outliers. In the Airbnb data, availability has none, since it is naturally bounded between zero and three sixty five, so a box plot is a useful sanity check as well as a finder.
#9
Let me explain the arithmetic. For price, the mean is about one fifty three and the standard deviation about two forty, so the Z-score limit is the mean plus three standard deviations, which is about eight hundred seventy three. A price of five hundred dollars has a Z of only about one point four five, so it is not flagged, even though it is nearly five times the median. The modified Z-score uses the median and the median absolute deviation, so the outliers cannot stretch it. The value zero point six seven four five is a scaling constant. Values above three point five are flagged.
#10
Let me explain how to compute it. In pandas, q1 equals df of price dot quantile of point two five, and q3 with point seven five. The interquartile range is q3 minus q1. The upper fence is q3 plus one point five times the interquartile range, and the lower fence is q1 minus one point five times it. Then df of price greater than the upper fence gives the flagged rows. Some practitioners use three times the interquartile range for extreme outliers only. Because the fences depend on the middle half of the data, they are not distorted by the outliers they are looking for.
#11
Let me interpret the table. Every column has a long right tail, so Z-score always finds fewer outliers than the interquartile range rule. For minimum nights, over six thousand rows versus only about three hundred, a huge difference. Neither number is the correct number of outliers. They are the result of two different definitions. So when you write a report, state which rule you used and why, and do not simply say that the data contains this many outliers.
#12
Let me describe masking with an analogy. Imagine measuring who is unusually tall in a class using a ruler that stretches whenever a very tall person stands next to it. The tall ones make the ruler longer, so they seem less unusual. That is exactly what happens: outliers inflate the standard deviation, which widens the Z-score limit. The interquartile range rule uses a ruler that does not stretch. In practice, this means that on skewed data like prices, incomes and waiting times, the interquartile range is the safer rule.
#13
Let me explain the settings. In scikit-learn, IsolationForest takes contamination, the share of rows you expect to be outliers, say point oh two. LocalOutlierFactor takes n neighbours, such as twenty, and you should scale features first. DBSCAN takes eps and min samples. For each model, you call fit predict on the scaled features, and the output has minus one for outliers. Always look at the flagged rows with the original unscaled values, because that is how you decide if they make sense.
#14
Let me give practical advice on choosing. If the question is whether the price is unusual, a rule on the price column is enough. If the question is whether this listing is strange given its price, reviews, availability and room type all together, a model helps. Remember that Isolation Forest will always flag the percentage you ask for, even if the data has no real outliers. So you must look at the flagged rows and decide whether they are meaningful. The model suggests, and you decide.
#15
Let me add a short note on each consequence. A wrong mean misleads the reader. An inflated spread makes everything look more variable than it is. A skew of nineteen breaks methods that assume a bell shape. A regression line can be tilted by a single row, which is why we plot residuals. Distance-based methods like K nearest neighbours and K-Means give far too much weight to a few extreme values. And mean squared error squares each error, so one big miss can dominate the score. Seeing this list, it is clear why outliers must be understood before modelling.
#16
Let me explain each fix in a sentence you can use. Removing deletes the row, so use it only for clear errors. Capping, also called clipping, moves every value above the fence down to the fence. Winsorising does the same at the first and ninety-ninth percentiles. Median replacement is simple, but flattens variation. K nearest neighbours or regression imputation estimates a plausible value from other columns. The log transform compresses a long tail while keeping every row. And a flag column keeps the information that the row was unusual. In practice you will often combine, for example flag and cap.
#17
Let me turn this into a decision routine. Look at the row. Is the value possible? If not, it is an error, so correct it from another source, or set it to missing and treat it later, or remove the row. Is it possible but extreme? Keep it, and then ask whether your method is sensitive, in which case cap, winsorise or transform. Is it capped by the data source? Flag it. Always write down what you did and how many rows were affected. In the lab, we apply this routine to the Airbnb data and then repeat it on a second dataset. Thank you for watching.
