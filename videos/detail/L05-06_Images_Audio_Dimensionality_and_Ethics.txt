#0
Let me tell you what is special about this lecture. So far the data we looked at came in tables. Today you will see that photos, voices and even documents can be turned into tables too, with an enormous number of columns. That one idea explains why modern machine learning is powerful, and also why it is difficult, which leads us to the curse of dimensionality. We finish with a topic every data scientist must take seriously: bias and ethics in data.
#1
Let me make this concrete. A tiny handwritten digit from the MNIST dataset is twenty eight pixels by twenty eight pixels, which is seven hundred eighty four numbers per image. Ten seconds of speech recorded at sixteen thousand samples per second is a hundred sixty thousand numbers. A short review with a vocabulary of twenty thousand words becomes twenty thousand counts, most of them zero. The table has one row per image, sound or document, and a very large number of columns. Keep that picture in mind through the rest of the video.
#2
Let me add a small experiment you can try. In Python, open any photo with PIL, convert it to grey, and call numpy dot array on it. You will see a matrix of whole numbers between zero and two fifty five. Change a few values, show it again, and you will see the picture change. Colour images have three such matrices stacked, one each for red, green and blue. A phone photo of twelve megapixels is therefore thirty six million numbers. Nothing in the numbers says there is a face; that has to be learned.
#3
Let me explain why the histogram of brightness is useful. If most of the pixels are near zero, the image is very dark. A tall bar at two fifty five means parts are over-exposed, so detail is lost. Plot one histogram per class and compare. If cat photos are mostly bright and dog photos mostly dark, a model may learn the lighting and not the animal. The checks on this slide, equal sizes, balanced classes and no duplicates between train and test, take minutes and often reveal the real problem in an image project.
#4
Let me explain the numbers. At forty four thousand one hundred samples per second, one minute of music is over two and a half million numbers. Human speech does not need that detail, so sixteen thousand is common for speech recognition. A waveform plot shows loudness over time, but not pitch or words. To see frequencies you compute a spectrogram, which is a picture of how the sound's frequencies change over time, and that turns an audio problem into an image problem. In the lab you will draw both a waveform and a histogram of an audio signal.
#5
Let me give you a little warning before we start. This part is not about a bug in any code. It is about a property of space itself. The same properties that make images and audio so rich, the huge number of numbers per item, are the ones that make learning from them hard when you do not have enough examples. Keep the dartboard from the first lecture in mind: more detail does not automatically mean more reliable answers.
#6
Let me give examples. For a student, the dimensions might be age, marks, attendance and hours of sleep, which is four. For a house, area, rooms, age and distance to the station. For an image, each pixel is a dimension. Remember also that when we say high-dimensional we do not mean a number like a thousand exactly. It is relative to the number of rows you have. Fifty columns with only a hundred rows is already a high-dimensional problem.
#7
Let me do the arithmetic aloud. If you want just ten distinct values along each feature and you want every combination to be represented, then with one feature you need ten points. With two features, ten times ten is a hundred. With three, a thousand. With ten features, ten billion. No dataset has that many rows. So as you add columns, the data you have covers a smaller and smaller fraction of the possible combinations, and the model must guess more and more about places it has never seen.
#8
Let me explain with a picture. Put a thousand points on a line, and they are packed closely. Spread the same thousand points over a square, and they are farther apart. Spread them in a cube, and the cube is mostly empty. In a hundred dimensions, the points are like a few grains of sand in a huge hall. For a model, that means that for any new point, there are very few training points nearby to learn from, so predictions become unreliable.
#9
Let me say more about nearest neighbours. Suppose you compute the distance from one point to all others. In two dimensions some are very near and some very far, so the nearest is clearly special. In a thousand dimensions, the nearest and farthest distances are almost the same, so the idea of a neighbour loses its meaning. This is why K nearest neighbours, K means clustering and many other distance-based methods slow down or fail as the number of features grows, unless you reduce the dimensions first.
#10
Let me extend the analogy. Think of looking for a friend in a lift lobby, then a whole campus, then a whole city, and then a thousand parallel cities. Each extra dimension multiplies the number of places to check. That is why a few well-chosen features usually beat a large number of poorly chosen ones. It is also the reason that experienced data scientists spend more time deciding what to include than which algorithm to use.
#11
Let me explain each remedy in a sentence you can use in an interview. Feature selection throws away columns that do not help. Dimensionality reduction, such as principal component analysis, combines correlated columns into a few new ones, and you will practise it in module four. Regularisation shrinks the influence of weak features, which is what ridge and lasso regression do. And collecting more relevant data helps, but because the space grows exponentially, you rarely get enough, so reduce the dimension first.
#12
Let me say what to do in practice. Before you add a column, ask whether it carries new information. Remove columns that are almost constant or that are copies of another. Check whether a few features already explain most of the variation. And when you plot, remember that you are only ever seeing two or three of the dimensions at a time, so two points that look far apart on the screen may be close in the full space, and the reverse.
#13
Let me read the numbers again. With ten measurements per axis, six features need ten to the power six, which is a million points. A twenty eight by twenty eight image has seven hundred eighty four features, so the number of cells is ten to the power seven hundred eighty four, more than the number of atoms in the universe. That is why learning from images works only because real images occupy a tiny, structured part of that space, and the models are built to find that part.
#14
Let me explain the volume point, which surprises many students. In a square, most of the area is in the middle. In a ten-dimensional cube, almost all the volume is in a thin shell near the surface, so randomly placed points are almost always near an edge or a corner. Next, when there are more columns than rows, a model can fit the training data perfectly, even if the labels are random, which is overfitting in its purest form. These are the reasons behind the first responses listed on the slide.
#15
Let me explain this with an example. Suppose a hiring model is trained on the past ten years of hiring decisions at a company. If that company mostly hired men for technical roles, the data records that history, and the model learns it as if it were a rule. Nobody wrote a rule saying prefer men, yet the pattern is in the data. Similarly, labels written by tired reviewers are noisy. And a model that is deployed changes what people do, which changes the next dataset. Bias is therefore a loop, not a single step.
#16
Let me explain the four questions with practical examples. Consent: a fitness app collected steps for tracking, and now someone wants to use the same data for insurance pricing. Privacy: even without names, a date of birth, postal code and gender together can identify most people. Fairness: a model with ninety percent accuracy overall might be ninety five percent accurate for one group and sixty percent for another. Consequence: if a loan is wrongly refused, can the person find out why and ask for a review? Always ask these before building anything.
#17
Let me explain the datasheet idea. In a short document, note where the data came from, who collected it and when, how people were selected, what is missing, and what you removed and why. Researchers call this a datasheet for datasets. It takes half an hour, and it protects your reader and you. In your own assignments, add a limitations paragraph that begins with this dataset does not tell us. Examiners and employers both value that sentence, because it shows judgement.
#18
Let me close with a short exercise. Take any small photo and convert it to grey, then print its shape and draw its pixel histogram. Take a short audio clip and plot its waveform. Then choose one dataset from Kaggle and write four lines on possible bias, covering who is in it, who might be missing, how labels were made, and what harm a wrong prediction could cause. In the lab you will do the image and audio parts in code. Thank you for watching.
