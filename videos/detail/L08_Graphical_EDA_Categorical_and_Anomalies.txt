#0
Let me explain how to choose the number of bins. If you use too few bins, a histogram hides the shape. If you use too many, it looks like noise. A good starting point is the square root of the number of rows, and seaborn picks a sensible default with its automatic rule. Try three different values and compare. A histogram is also the best place to spot problems, such as a spike at zero, gaps, or a hard cut-off at the maximum.
#1
Let me read these histograms more closely. The flat ones suggest that every order quantity from one to fifty is about equally likely, which is unusual in real life and may mean the data is synthetic or sampled evenly. The tall single bar in sales means that almost all orders are small, and a few big ones stretch the axis so far that the bars for them are too small to see. When this happens, plotting the logarithm of the values, or limiting the axis, shows more detail.
#2
Let me explain the idea of a kernel density estimate. Imagine placing a small smooth bump at every data point and adding them all up. Where points are close together, the bumps pile up and the curve is high. The width of each bump is called the bandwidth, and it plays the role that bin width plays in a histogram. In seaborn you draw it with kdeplot, and you can fill the area under the curve. Be careful that the curve may extend past the real limits of the data, for example into negative prices, because the smoothing does not know the limits.
#3
Let me add a point about interpretation. In a density plot the vertical axis is not a count, it is a density, so only the shape and relative heights matter. When two features have very different scales, plotting them on the same axis is misleading, so we draw one panel per feature. For profit, the sharp narrow peak at zero, with thin tails on both sides, is a fair picture of an enormous number of small orders and a few extreme ones.
#4
Let me remind you how to read the box. The line in the middle is the median. The box is from the first to the third quartile, so its height is the interquartile range. The whiskers extend to the furthest points that are inside the fences, which are one point five times the interquartile range beyond the box. Points outside are drawn as dots. One limitation is that a boxplot cannot show whether the data has two humps, because it only uses five numbers.
#5
Let me explain what the crushed boxes mean. When the box and whiskers are squeezed against zero and a long trail of dots extends to the right, the axis has been stretched to include the biggest outliers, so the ordinary values are compressed into a tiny space. This is a visual warning that a transformation or a separate analysis of the extremes is needed. For profit, the dots on both sides show that outliers are not just big wins but also big losses.
#6
Let me explain how a violin is built. Take the density curve, mirror it, and put a small boxplot inside. The wider part shows where more data lies. It combines two of the earlier plots, so it is a useful single figure when you have space for only one. It is especially good when comparing groups, for example profit by customer segment, because you see the shape of each group's distribution side by side.
#7
Let me note the lesson in these violins. For uniform data like order quantity, the violin looks like a rectangle. For symmetric data like profit near zero, it is a narrow vertical spindle. For extremely skewed data, the violin degenerates into a needle, because the density is so concentrated that the rest of the shape is invisible. When you see a needle, the right next step is not a prettier plot, but a transformation or a cap on the axis.
#8
Let me explain the logic. Suppose you draw profit for the three product categories on one density plot. If the three curves are almost the same, product category tells you little about profit. If they are clearly different in centre or spread, then product category is a useful feature, and you should investigate it further. This is an informal check, and later you can back it up with a statistical test. It is also a quick way to generate hypotheses, such as technology orders are riskier than furniture orders.
#9
Let me interpret this carefully. All three curves are centred near zero, so the typical order loses or gains little in every category. But technology has the widest spread, which means it has both the biggest wins and the biggest losses. The average shows that the wins win in the end, with technology averaging four hundred twenty nine dollars per order against sixty eight for furniture. But remember what we learned earlier: with skewed data, the mean can be dominated by a few large orders. So check the median and the number of loss-making orders before drawing a business conclusion.
#10
Let me connect this with common sense. Delivery trucks carry large, heavy items, such as furniture and bulk goods, so their shipping costs are higher and more variable. Air shipping is used for small, light, urgent items. The data confirms the pattern we would expect. This is a good moment to remember that exploratory analysis is not only about finding surprises. It is also about checking that the data matches reality, because if it did not, that would be a sign of a data problem.
#11
Let me restate this in plain words. If you only look at the numbers, you know that unit price is extreme, and by how much. If you only look at the picture, you see that something is extreme, but you cannot put a figure on it. Together, you know what the extreme is, how big it is, and where the problem lies, in a small set of very expensive products. This leads to a practical decision. For a model, you might transform the price. For a business report, you might analyse premium products separately.
#12
Let me say why this is a reassuring case. Order quantity is spread evenly between one and fifty, so the mean and median agree, the boxplot has no dots and the violin is a rectangle. Negative kurtosis of about minus one point two is the exact value for a perfectly uniform distribution, so the number and the picture agree beautifully. This is rare in real data and it is a hint that this column may be synthetic. Real order quantities are usually concentrated at small values.
#13
Let me close this loop with a rule. Use numbers when you need precision and a record, for example in a table in your report. Use pictures when you need to see shape, or when you want to spot problems you did not think to check. When they agree, you can summarise with confidence. When they disagree in detail, or surprise you, dig deeper. A good assignment always shows both, with one sentence explaining what each adds.
#14
Let me explain how to build one. In pandas, value counts gives the counts, and passing normalize equal to True gives proportions. Multiply by one hundred for percentages. Sort the table so the most common category is first. Also look at the smallest categories. If a category has only a handful of rows, any average computed for it is unreliable. You may need to merge it with similar categories, or leave it out of grouped comparisons, and say so in your report.
#15
Let me explain what we learn here. Office supplies are more than half of the orders, so any overall average is mostly an average of office supplies. Regular air makes up three quarters of orders, so conclusions about express air are based on far fewer rows. Customer segments are fairly balanced, which is good for comparisons. Whenever you compare groups later, remember the group sizes, because a difference between a big group and a small one is less certain than it looks.
#16
Let me add a few tips. Always order the bars sensibly: by size for nominal categories, and in natural order for ordinal ones such as low, medium and high. Start the vertical axis at zero, because bars encode value by length, and a truncated axis exaggerates differences. Add the values as labels if the exact numbers matter. In seaborn, countplot makes a bar plot of counts in one line, and barplot lets you show the mean of another variable for each category.
#17
Let me interpret these three bar plots. The first shows dominance by one category. The second shows a steep drop that reflects a default option, since most customers just accept regular shipping. The third shows a more even picture. Bar plots make the size of the differences obvious, but they do not tell you why. For the why, you would combine them with numeric summaries, for example the average profit per segment, or a question to the business team about how orders are assigned.
#18
Let me add some guidance on when to avoid pies. Our eyes are good at comparing lengths along a line and not good at comparing angles or areas. So if two slices are nearly equal in size, a pie will not help you see which is bigger, whereas a bar chart will. Use a pie only for a small number of slices, when the message is that one part is a large share of the whole. Never use a three-dimensional pie, because the perspective distorts the sizes.
#19
Let me interpret this. The pie confirms that office supplies make up well over half the orders, and it makes the share easy to feel. However, notice that the pie gives nothing about profit or sales value. Office supplies may be the most frequent, but technology may bring more revenue per order. Always ask what the chart is measuring, orders, revenue or profit, because the same categories can rank differently under each.
#20
Let me explain the numbers on this slide. A profit of twenty seven thousand dollars is a very large order, and a loss of fourteen thousand dollars is a very large loss, and both are far outside the fences. They are not data errors, because the data have complete order records. So we keep them, but we make sure our summaries and models handle them. In pandas, you can compute the fences in three lines and filter the rows outside them to inspect them one by one.
#21
Let me add how to use this. Profit has twenty percent of its rows flagged, which is a high share. It means the distribution is very wide compared with its middle half, so the fence rule flags many legitimate values. That is a clue that a single fence may not suit this feature, and that you should consider grouping by category before applying the rule. A rate this high should make you think about the data and not only about the outliers. Discount, with only three flagged rows, shows what a well-behaved column looks like.
