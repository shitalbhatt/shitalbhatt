#1
Let me explain why this is a bigger problem than it looks. Imagine a class survey on income in which some students leave the answer blank. If you delete those students, you lose information about who they are. If you fill every blank with the class average, everybody in the blank group suddenly has the same income, which is false and makes the data look more uniform than it is. Twenty percent of rows is too many to simply drop, and too many to fill carelessly. So the first step in any missing data problem is to ask why the value is missing.
#2
Let me give one more example of each. MCAR: a lab instrument that randomly fails on a few readings, with no connection to the patient or the value. MAR: older customers are less likely to fill the email field, so the missing email depends on age, which we have recorded. MNAR: people with very high incomes are less likely to report income, so the missingness depends on the very value that is missing. The danger is that MNAR cannot be detected from the data you have. You only suspect it, using knowledge of how the data was collected.
#3
Let me explain the logic of the proof. We take all the rows where reviews per month is missing, and look at the number of reviews in those rows. If the gaps were random, we would see a mix of zero and non-zero review counts. Instead, all ten thousand one hundred sixteen have zero reviews. A listing that no one has reviewed cannot have a reviews per month rate. So the correct value for these rows is, logically, zero. This is the sort of reasoning that no imputation method can replace. Also remember that MCAR can only be suspected, and never proved.
#4
Let me explain the first lab steps in code. df equals pd dot read csv with the file name. df dot shape confirms forty-nine thousand one hundred ninety-five rows and sixteen columns. df dot isnull dot sum gives the count of missing values in each column, and dividing by the length and multiplying by one hundred gives percentages. Only four columns have gaps. Two of them, last review and reviews per month, have about twenty percent. The other two have just a handful. This tells us where to focus.
#5
Let me explain how to build this crosstab. pd dot crosstab takes two boolean series: the first is df of reviews per month dot isnull, and the second is df of number of reviews equal to zero. The result is a two by two table. If a row is missing, the review count must be zero, and the cell for missing with non-zero reviews is empty. The cell for not missing with zero reviews is empty too. A table like this is stronger than any correlation, because it shows a logical rule in the data.
#6
Let me explain how to find them. For zeros, count df of price equal to zero. For sentinels, look at the maximum of each numeric column, and at value counts of the top values, where nine ninety-nine stands out. For text, check value counts of host name for entries like Nan. Then replace them using df dot replace, or np dot nan, so that pandas treats them as missing. Be careful with judgement, since zero is a perfectly valid number of reviews or of available days, but not a valid price.
#7
Let me explain the trade-offs more. Simple methods are fast and easy to explain, and they work for a small share of missing values that are missing at random. They hurt when many values are missing, because they flatten the distribution. K nearest neighbours uses other columns, so it respects relations in the data, but needs scaled features, and gets slow on large datasets. Regression imputation is easy to interpret, but its quality depends on how well the other columns predict the target, which we can check with R squared. The right choice depends on how much is missing, and why.
#8
Let me explain what variance retained means. We compute the variance of the observed values, which is two point eight two. After filling, we compute the variance of the whole filled column and divide by the observed variance. Mean fill keeps seventy-nine percent, so about a fifth of the spread has been lost. The reason is that the gaps now all have the same value, which sits at the centre. In pandas, df of column dot fillna with the median is one line. Remember that the median is robust when the data is skewed.

The nearest neighbours found by KNN have few reviews, so it fills small values, and it learned the pattern without being told, because the zero-review rows are close to each other in the feature space.
#10
Let me explain the regression approach. Take the rows where reviews per month is known, and fit a linear regression using other columns as predictors, such as price, availability, number of reviews and location. Then predict for the missing rows, and clip negative predictions to zero. The R squared of point three three says that the model explains a third of the variation, which is modest. It predicts an average of point seven eight for the zero-review rows, which we know should be zero, so here regression is clearly worse than the simple logical rule.
#11
Let me explain how to read these three histograms. On the left is the original distribution, with its natural shape. In the middle, after mean filling, there is a very tall bar at one value, which is the average, because over ten thousand rows received the same number. On the right, after KNN, the shape looks like the original. A good habit is to draw this comparison for every imputation you do, since a statistic like the variance may hide how strange the picture looks.
#12
Let me explain how to build the table. Create a data frame with one row per method, and columns for the mean, standard deviation and variance retained. Sort by variance retained. Remember that a high retained variance does not prove that the fill is right. It only shows that you did not flatten the data. The best check is always your understanding of why the value is missing. In our case, the rule that no reviews means a rate of zero is the best of all, and KNN comes closest to it.
