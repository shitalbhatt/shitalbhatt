Let me explain why we begin by looking for causes. Before any rule runs, you read the data. Filter the rows with price equal to zero and look at them. Print the three highest prices and read the descriptions. Count how many listings have more than three sixty five minimum nights, which is impossible for a normal stay. #2
Let me give the code. z equals df of the columns minus df of the columns dot mean, divided by df of the columns dot std. Take the absolute value with dot abs, compare it with three, and sum to count. In pandas, the whole table of true and false values is created at once, which is called broadcasting. Adding axis equals one to any gives the rows that are flagged in at least one column. Remember that pandas dot std uses n minus one by default, which is fine for our purpose. Number of reviews has the most Z-score outliers, because its tail is very long.
#3
Let me give the code. q1 equals df of the columns dot quantile of point two five, and q3 with point seven five, each giving one value per column. The interquartile range is q3 minus q1. The lower and upper fences are computed in the same way. Then df less than lower or df greater than upper gives a table of true and false, and sum counts per column. The row-wise any call shows that thirty-eight percent of homes are outliers in at least one column. Because that is so many, removing every flagged row would destroy more than a third of the data, and it is not a sensible approach.
#4
Let me explain the settings. IsolationForest with n estimators of one hundred, contamination equal to point oh two, and random state forty two. Fit predict on the six features gives minus one for outliers. Because contamination is two percent, you always get about two percent flagged. The interesting part is looking at who they are. The flagged homes have a median minimum stay of twenty-nine nights and are available almost the whole year, which is typical of company-managed long-stay apartments. This is a multivariate outlier: no single column is extreme, but the combination is unusual.
#5
Let me explain why this is useful. A twenty-dollar private room in Staten Island is not extreme in any absolute sense, because twenty dollars is well inside the overall price range. But compared with its neighbours, it is surprising. Interquartile range on the price column can never flag it, as the lower fence is negative. So local methods like LOF, which compare each point to its neighbours, find a different kind of outlier. We use latitude, longitude and log price, scaled, so the neighbours are geographic and price is on a comparable scale.
#6
Let me explain how to read this comparison. If several methods flag the same row, it is a strong candidate and deserves a close look. If only one flags it, find out what that method is measuring. DBSCAN depends heavily on eps. A small eps flags many points as noise, and a large eps flags few, so always check a few values. Remember that the aim is not to find a single correct list. It is to understand which rows are unusual in which way, and then decide what to do with each type.
#7
Let me explain the code for the fixes. Capping uses df of price dot clip with the lower and upper fences. Winsorising uses clip with the first and ninety-ninth percentiles, which you get from quantile. Median replacement uses where, keeping values inside the fences and replacing others with the median. The group median uses groupby with transform. Log uses np dot log one p. Compare the maximum price after each version. Capping gives three thirty four, winsorising gives seven ninety nine, and removal simply deletes the rows. Choose according to the cause, as we discussed in the lecture.
#8
Let me explain the steps. Make a copy of the price column, and set the flagged values to NaN. Then build a feature matrix with latitude, longitude, minimum nights, availability, reviews, and one-hot encoded room type, and use KNNImputer with five neighbours, or fit a LinearRegression on the rows with known price and predict for the missing ones. The advantage is that an apartment in a pricey area gets a higher replacement than one in a cheap area. The warning about forty-seven cents is real: a linear model can predict impossible values, so clip the results to a sensible range.
#9
Let me explain how to read this table. If the median hardly changes but the mean and standard deviation drop sharply, then outliers were the cause of the shift. The median was already robust. This is a neat way to demonstrate in your report that the outliers mattered, and it is also a way to choose between fixes. A fix that gives a skew of about one and a standard deviation of under eighty-five dollars, with all rows kept, is a good practical choice for this data.
#10
Let me add how to read the skew table. A skew above one is still lopsided, but it is far better than nineteen. For host listings count, one company with three hundred twenty-seven homes is still enormously above the typical host with one or two. This is the sign of a different population: a business and not an individual. A better fix may be to analyse businesses and individual hosts separately, or to add a flag, rather than forcing the value down.
#11
Let me explain why a log axis is used. On the raw scale, one box plot with a maximum of ten thousand dollars squeezes all the others into a thin line. On the log scale, equal distances mean equal ratios, so every version is visible. When you compare the histograms, notice that the capped version has a small spike at the cap, three thirty four, because all the larger values were moved to that point. That is a visible trace of the fix, and you should mention it in your report.
#12
Let me explain how this experiment was designed. We split the data once, with the same random state, so that every model sees the same training and test rows. We fit the same linear regression every time. Only the treatment of the outliers changes. That is what makes the comparison fair. The R squared rising from point one two to point four four tells us the model explains much more of the price variation, and the mean absolute error falling from seventy-one to about thirty-six dollars tells us the typical error was halved. Next, note that we evaluate on the test set, so this is not just fitting noise.
