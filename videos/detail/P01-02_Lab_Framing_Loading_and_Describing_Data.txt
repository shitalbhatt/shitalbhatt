#0
Let me tell you how to use this lab video. Open a new Jupyter notebook or Google Colab notebook, and follow along by typing every line. We will use a small dataset of customer spending, but you can replace it with any table that interests you. The aim is to build a routine you can use on every dataset in this course: frame the question, load the data, name the variables, describe the numbers, and look at the shape.
#1
Let me explain the four stages. In stage one, you write down what you are asking, then load the file and check what arrived. In stage two, you decide the outcome variable and its possible explainers. In stage three, you compute summaries. In stage four, you draw plots and a pivot table. Notice the order: numbers come after the question and the plots come with the numbers, never instead of them. If you ever feel lost in a data project, come back to this path and ask which stage you are at.
#2
Let me show you how a good problem statement sounds. A weak statement is: analyse customer data. It does not say what to look for. A strong statement is: do customers who received the discount email spend more per month than customers who did not, in the last quarter? It names the row, which is a customer-month, the outcome, which is monthly spend, and the comparison. The last test, will the answer change an action, is the most important one. If nobody would act differently whatever the answer, the question is not worth the effort.
#3
Let me go through the five lines in order. Read C S V loads the file into a data frame, and you should check the path and the separator if it fails. The shape attribute gives rows and columns, and you compare it with what the data provider promised. Head shows the first five rows, so you see real values. The dtypes attribute shows whether each column is a number, text or a date, and a number stored as text is a very common surprise. Finally isna dot sum counts the missing values per column. If any of these five surprises you, stop and investigate before doing anything else.
#4
Let me give two more examples. In a study of exam results, the dependent variable is the final score, and the independent variables could be attendance, hours of study, and the number of practice tests taken. In a telecom dataset, the dependent variable could be whether the customer left, and the independent variables could be the plan, the monthly bill and the number of complaints. Naming them early keeps your plots focused, and it also protects you from a classic mistake, which is accidentally including the outcome, or something that reveals it, among the explainers.
#5
Let me explain what the two numbers are telling us. The mean is thirty eight dollars sixty three cents and the median is thirty four dollars fifty nine cents. The gap is about four dollars, which is more than ten percent of the median. Most customers spend close to thirty four dollars, but a smaller group spending a lot pulls the mean up. In pandas, df of the column dot mean and dot median give these in one line each. A good habit is to print both together every time, because the gap between them is the quickest hint of skewness.
#6
Let me explain the two spreads. The standard deviation is about twenty dollars fifty five cents and the interquartile range is twenty three dollars fifty cents. They are of a similar size here, which is reasonable for a moderately skewed column. In pandas, dot std gives the standard deviation, and for the interquartile range you take dot quantile of point seven five minus dot quantile of point two five. Remember also that variance is just the standard deviation squared, so it is in squared dollars and is harder to interpret, which is why we usually quote the standard deviation instead.
#7
Let me turn this into a rule you can apply without a plot. Take the mean and subtract the median. If the answer is clearly positive, expect a right tail. If it is clearly negative, expect a left tail. If it is near zero, expect balance. Of course, this is only a hint, and some unusual shapes can fool it, for example when the data has two groups. For a more precise number, call dot skew in pandas, where a value near zero means symmetric and values beyond plus or minus one mean a strong skew. Then confirm by looking at the histogram.
#8
Let me add how to draw each view in seaborn. For a histogram, use sns dot histplot with the column, and try different numbers of bins, because too few hide the shape and too many show noise. For a boxplot, use sns dot boxplot, and read the box as the middle half, the line as the median, and the dots as flagged points. For a density curve, use sns dot kdeplot, or add kde equals True to the histogram. Put them side by side in one figure with plt dot subplots, so a reader can compare the three at once.
#9
Let me explain the pivot table call. In pandas you write pd dot pivot table, give the data frame, then say which column becomes the rows, which becomes the columns, which holds the values, and which aggregation function to use, such as mean, sum or count. If you are used to Excel, this is exactly the same idea as a pivot table there. In the example, a single overall average of sales would have mixed East and South together and hidden that the best-selling product reverses between them. Whenever your question has the word by in it, such as sales by region by product, think pivot table.
