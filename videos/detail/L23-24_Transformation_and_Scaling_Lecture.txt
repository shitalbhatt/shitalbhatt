#0
Let me tell you what this module four is about. Until now we studied and cleaned data. From here on, we prepare it for modelling, which means reshaping it so that a model can learn from it easily. Today's two lectures deal with the shape and the units of numeric features. Both are simple, but they are used in almost every machine learning pipeline, and mixing them up is a very common mistake. Remember the one-line rule: transforming changes the shape, scaling changes the units.
#1
Let me explain with a small example. Imagine one column of incomes in lakhs, strongly skewed, and another column of ages in years. A transformation, like a logarithm, would make the income column closer to a bell shape. A scaler, like the standard scaler, would then put income and age on the same footing, so that a distance-based model does not treat income as more important just because its numbers are larger. They solve different problems, and for skewed data we usually need both, in that order.
#2
Let me explain how to read the dataset description. A block group is a small census area, with typically a few hundred to a few thousand people. The seven features we transform are all positive numbers, which matters because Box-Cox needs positive values. We leave longitude and latitude alone because they are coordinates, and transforming them has no meaning. And the top-coding of house value at five hundred thousand and one is a reminder from the outlier lecture that some extreme values are artefacts of how the data was recorded.
#3
Let me explain each reason with an example. Squashed plots: a scatterplot of population against house value looks like a vertical smear near zero, and you cannot see the relationship. Model assumptions: linear regression does not strictly require a normal feature, but it is happier with roughly symmetric features and residuals. False outliers: in a long tail, the interquartile range rule flags many ordinary large values, and after a transform they are no longer flagged. Mixed units: K-means or K nearest neighbours would see population in thousands as far more important than income in tens of thousands of dollars.
#4
Let me give the practical meaning. In pandas, df dot skew gives these numbers in one line. Population has a skew of almost five, which is extreme. Total rooms, bedrooms and households are all measures of size, and they behave alike, which is why they will need the same treatment. Housing median age is symmetric already, so we leave it alone. Notice that this is a per-feature decision. Not every column needs a transform, and a good analyst starts by measuring and not by transforming everything.
#5
Let me explain with an analogy. Transforming is like bending a ruler. Big values get squeezed more than small ones, so the picture changes, but the order of the values is still the same: the largest is still the largest. Scaling is like changing the unit from centimetres to metres. The picture is identical, only the numbers on the axis change. This is why a scaler cannot fix skew, and why a histogram before and after scaling looks exactly the same. Remember the order for skewed data: transform first, then scale.
#6
Let me give a small calculation. Take the values one, ten, one hundred, one thousand and ten thousand. After log base ten of one plus x, they become about point three, one point one, two, three and four. A range of ten thousand has been squeezed into a range of four. In pandas, you write np dot log one p of the column. The caution in the lecture is important: log has one fixed strength, so applied to features that were only moderately skewed, it overshoots, and a right skew becomes a left skew. And on a symmetric column like age, it distorts a perfectly good shape.
#7
Let me explain how to use it. In scipy, stats dot boxcox of the column returns the transformed values and the best lambda. In scikit-learn, PowerTransformer with method box-cox does it for many columns at once, and stores the lambdas. A lambda near one means the feature needed almost no change, as for housing median age. A lambda near zero means it behaved like a log case, as for income. To interpret: lambda one half is a square root, and lambda minus one is a reciprocal. If your data has zeros or negatives, switch to Yeo-Johnson, which is available in the same class.
#8
Let me add what each means in practice. The standard scaler is the default for most models, including linear models, support vector machines and principal component analysis, which assume features on comparable scales. Min-max is common for neural networks and image pixels, where you want a fixed zero to one range, but it is fragile with outliers. Robust is the safe choice for outlier-heavy features. In every case, fit the scaler on the training data only, and then apply it to the test data, otherwise information leaks from the test set.
#9
Let me explain how to use these checks as a routine. Compute skewness and kurtosis before and after, count outliers by the interquartile range rule, and draw the histogram and box plot for each version. If skewness moves close to zero and the histogram looks like a bell, the transform worked. If the skew overshoots, the transform was too strong. If new outliers appear in a column that was fine, the transform was wrong for that column. In the lab, you will do all of this for seven features, in a table. Thank you for watching.
