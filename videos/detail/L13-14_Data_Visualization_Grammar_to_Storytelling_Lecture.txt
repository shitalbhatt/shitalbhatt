#0
Let me tell you why this lecture matters even though you have already drawn many charts. Drawing a chart is easy. Drawing one that is correct, clear and persuasive is a skill. In this lecture we learn a way of thinking about charts, called the grammar of graphics, then we look at how charts can mislead, how to draw non-numeric data, how dashboards are planned, and how to build a story. Everything here directly supports Assignment one and your final project.
#1
Let me add a way to remember. First comes how a chart is made, then how to make it honest, then how to draw unusual data, then how to put several charts together, and finally how to give them a message. Think of it as grammar, honesty, variety, layout and story. If you are ever stuck when preparing a presentation, go through these five and ask which one is weak.
#2
Let me explain this with the correlation of point eight two, a surprisingly high figure for all four datasets. Francis Anscombe created these in nineteen seventy three, precisely to show that numbers alone mislead. The first looks like a normal noisy line. The second is a perfect curve, where a straight line is the wrong model. The third has a line with one outlier pulling the fit. The fourth has all points at the same x except one, which alone creates the correlation. The conclusion is practical: never report a correlation without a scatterplot next to it.
#3
Let me use an example. Suppose you make a scatterplot of discount against profit, with colour showing the category. The data is the Superstore table. The aesthetics map discount to x, profit to y, and category to colour. The geometry is points. The scales and theme decide that the axes are in dollars, the legend is on the right, and the background is white. Libraries like ggplot in R and plotnine in Python follow this grammar explicitly, and seaborn follows it loosely. Once you think in layers, changing a chart is easy, because you change just one layer.
#4
Let me explain with a simple test. Compare two bars of nearly the same height. You can tell which is taller at once, because length on a common baseline is very accurate. Now compare two pie slices of nearly the same size. It is difficult, because angles and areas are harder to judge. This ranking comes from research by Cleveland and McGill in the nineteen eighties. It does not mean pies are forbidden. It means that when the exact comparison is the message, use position or length, and use colour only for grouping.
#5
Let me read this like a menu. If you want to know what values are common, draw a distribution plot. If you want to know whether two numbers move together, draw a scatterplot. If you want to see change over time, draw a line. If you want to compare groups, use a bar or a box plot, and use the box plot when the distribution matters. To compare two categories, use a heatmap of the cross-tab, and for shares, a stacked bar or a treemap. Match the chart to the question, and not to what looks impressive.
#6
Let me give an example. Suppose you want to compare profit across four regions, split by three product categories. One plot with twelve colours would be unreadable. With facets, you draw three small box plots, one per category, each with four regions, all on the same scale. In seaborn, you write catplot or relplot with col equal to the category column. Keep the same axis limits across panels, which is the default, because that is what makes comparison honest.
#7
Let me explain how large the distortion is. If the axis starts at ninety five and the bars are at ninety six and one hundred, the second bar looks as if it is four times the first, but the real difference is about four percent. Readers judge a bar by its length, so cutting off the bottom changes the message. For line charts, where the position of the points matters and not the length, zooming in on a range is acceptable, but say so clearly in the axis label or note.
#8
Let me give one example for each. Dual axes: you can make two unrelated series, like ice cream sales and a company's share price, look perfectly in sync by choosing the two scales. Cherry-picking: showing temperature from only the three cold months to argue that it is cooling. Areas: drawing a circle with double the radius for a value that is double, which makes it look four times larger. Three-D: a three-D pie where the front slice looks bigger. And context: a chart showing a rise in complaints without saying whether the number of customers also rose.
#9
Let me give you a practical checklist before you share any chart. Does the title say what the reader should take away? Do the axes have names and units? Does the bar chart start at zero? Is the colour palette safe for colour-blind readers, for example blue and orange, rather than red and green? Have I removed everything that is not data? And have I said how many rows it is based on and where the data came from? If you can answer yes to all six, your chart is honest and ready.
#10
Let me explain each of these. Sort the bars by value so the ranking is obvious, and if there are many categories, use horizontal bars so the labels are readable. A treemap, available through the squarify library or plotly, packs rectangles whose areas match values, which is ideal for category and sub-category sales. For text, remove stopwords, count words, and show the top twenty as a bar chart, because exact counts are easier to read than a word cloud. For a network, the networkx library draws nodes and edges, for example customers connected by shared products.
#11
Let me explain how to plan one. Write down the audience and the decision first. If the audience is a sales director who must decide whether to cut discounts, the first row should show total profit, the share of loss-making orders and the average discount, as large numbers. Below, a trend of profit over time and a breakdown by region. Use the same colour for the same thing everywhere, so that blue always means Central, for instance. Tools such as Tableau, Power BI, or Python with Plotly Dash can build these, but the thinking is the same.
#12
Let me compare two titles for the same chart. A weak title is: average profit by region. A strong title is: Central region loses money on average while every other region makes a profit. The second tells the reader what to see. Then use colour to guide the eye: make all bars grey except the Central bar, which is in a strong colour, and add a short annotation on that bar. Remove gridlines and borders. After that, the reader gets the message in three seconds.
#13
Let me show how the three parts could look for the Superstore data. Context: the company sells office supplies, technology and furniture, and has grown steadily. Conflict: more than half of the orders lose money, and the loss is concentrated in heavily discounted orders. Resolution: cap discounts at a level the data supports, and test the change in one region first. Notice that each chart on your slides should fit into one of these parts. If a chart does not support any of them, remove it, however nice it looks.
#14
Let me add practical advice about Assignment one. Do not try to show everything you computed. Choose the findings that answer your problem statement and drop the rest to an appendix. Keep each slide to one message, with an insight title. Make sure every number on a slide can be traced back to a cell in your notebook. And practise your presentation aloud, because a clear story is easier to tell than to read. The marking will look for honest charts, correct interpretation and a clear recommendation.
