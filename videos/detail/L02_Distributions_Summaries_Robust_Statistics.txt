#0
In the last lecture we asked whether the sample can be trusted. In this lecture we assume the sample is fine and ask a different question: how do we describe what is inside it? The short answer is shape before numbers. Many beginners calculate an average first and look at a plot last, and that order hides mistakes. By the end of this video you will be able to read a histogram, name its skewness and tail weight, flag outliers with a rule you can defend, and choose between mean and median without guessing.
#1
Let me add a small story. Suppose a coaching class reports that the average score of its students is sixty. If everyone scored close to sixty, that number is a fair description. If half the students scored thirty and half scored ninety, the average is still sixty, but not one student is anywhere near it. And if most scored fifty and a few scored a hundred, the average is pulled up and flatters the class. One number, three different realities. That is why a good analyst always draws the histogram next to the average, because the plot is what tells you whether the average deserves your trust.
#2
Let me explain the three questions with an example from daily life. Think of the time your friends take to reach college every morning. Where is it? Most take about thirty minutes, that is the centre. How wide is it? Some take twenty, some take forty, that is the spread. What shape is it? Maybe one friend sometimes takes ninety minutes when the bus breaks down, that is a long tail. Mean, median and mode answer the first question. Range, standard deviation and the interquartile range answer the second. Skewness, kurtosis and unusual points answer the third. You need all three answers to describe a column honestly.
#3
Let me give you a simple way to remember the direction. The skew is named after the long tail, not after the hump. If the tail drags out to the right, towards large values, we say right-skewed, or positively skewed. Income, house prices, hospital bills, and the time you wait for a customer care call are all right-skewed, because there is a floor at zero but no ceiling. Marks in an easy exam are left-skewed, because most students are near the maximum and a few failed. Numerically, skewness is the average of the cubed standardised values. In pandas, the dot skew method gives it, and values beyond about plus or minus one suggest a strong skew.
#4
Let me show why the mean moves. Imagine ten people sit on a see-saw. The mean is the balance point, so one very heavy person far from the centre drags it a long way. The median is just the middle person in the queue, so it only notices where the person stands in the line, not how heavy they are. That is why in right-skewed data the order from low to high is mode, then median, then mean. When someone says the average salary in a city is high, ask for the median, because that is closer to what an ordinary person earns.
#5
Let me clear up a common confusion. Many books say kurtosis measures how peaked the curve is. That is not quite right. It measures how much of the variance comes from extreme values in the tails. The formula uses the fourth power of the standardised values, so a single far-away point contributes enormously. A normal curve has a kurtosis of three, and subtracting three gives the excess kurtosis, which is zero for a normal curve. Both pandas dot kurt and scipy kurtosis report the excess version by default, so do not compare them against three by mistake.
#6
Let me give examples for each family. Platykurtic means flat tails: the marks of a class where almost everyone is close to the average, or the result of rolling a fair die, which can never exceed six. Mesokurtic means the bell curve, our reference. Leptokurtic means fat tails: daily returns of a stock, insurance claims, or the number of people entering a railway station on festival days. In such data a day with an extreme value is not a surprise, it is expected now and then, and a risk estimate built only on the standard deviation will understate how bad things can get.
#7
Let me work a number through. Suppose the first quartile is twenty and the third quartile is forty. The interquartile range is forty minus twenty, which is twenty. Multiply by one point five to get thirty. The lower fence is twenty minus thirty, so minus ten, and the upper fence is forty plus thirty, so seventy. Any value above seventy is flagged. Notice the box plot draws exactly this: the box is Q one to Q three, the line inside is the median, and the whiskers reach the furthest points inside the fences. Points beyond the whiskers are drawn as dots. In pandas you can compute the quartiles with dot quantile of point two five and point seven five.
#8
Let me give an example for each reason. A mistake: an age of nine ninety nine, which is clearly a placeholder, so correct or remove it and write that down. Rare but real: one customer who buys a thousand units for a wedding. That row may be your most valuable customer, so removing it would hide the business. Wrong population: a shop that sells to individuals and to companies, where the company orders are ten times larger. Mixing them gives a confusing average, so you split the data and study each group separately. The golden rule is that you never delete a point just because it is inconvenient.
#9
Let me read the table in a practical way. If you need one number for the centre of tidy data, use the mean. If the data is skewed, use the median. Range is easy to explain but depends only on two points, so one outlier ruins it. Standard deviation is the standard choice for symmetric data, but because it squares the distances, an outlier affects it a lot. The interquartile range looks only at the middle half, so it is stable. A useful habit is to print the mean and median side by side. If they are far apart, you have just learned that the data is skewed or contaminated.
#10
Let me finish this example. With seven staff members the mean salary describes the room well. When the manager joins at four hundred thousand, the mean jumps to a figure that no staff member actually earns. This is the idea of a breakdown point, which tells you what fraction of the data can be bad before a statistic becomes useless. For the mean it is zero percent, because a single bad value can move it anywhere. For the median it is fifty percent, because you would need to corrupt half the data. That is why robust statistics matter whenever you do not completely control how the data was collected.
#11
Let me turn the rule into a quick routine. Step one, draw the histogram and the box plot. Step two, print the mean, the median, the standard deviation and the interquartile range. Step three, compare. If mean and median agree and no points are flagged, use mean and standard deviation. If they disagree, say so in your report and lead with the median. If a few points are far away and you do not know why, show both versions of the number, with and without those points, so your reader can see how much depends on them. Honest reporting is less about the number and more about showing how it was chosen.

You will then call dot skew and dot kurt to put numbers on what you saw, you will compute the one point five interquartile range fence, and you will compare the mean with the median column by column. #13
Let me explain the z-score mistake because it surprises people. In a sample of size n, the largest possible z-score is n minus one divided by the square root of n. For ten observations that is nine divided by about three point two, which is about two point eight. So with ten data points, no value can ever cross the usual cut-off of three, however extreme it is. The rule only becomes useful with larger samples. Another mistake worth repeating is sentinel values. If a column uses minus one or nine ninety nine to mean not recorded, they must be converted to missing values before any statistic is computed.
