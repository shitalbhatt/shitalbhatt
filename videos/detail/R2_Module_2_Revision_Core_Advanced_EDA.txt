#1
Let me add a small way to remember. Numbers give precision, plots give shape, and you need both. Skewness, kurtosis and the interquartile range are the numbers. Histogram, boxplot, violin and density are the plots. Frequency tables and bar plots are for categories. If you can describe a column with a sentence containing one number and one picture, you have understood the point of these two lectures.
#2
Let me add a hook for each. Covariance gives direction in confusing units. Correlation puts it on a scale from minus one to one. Pearson is for straight lines, Spearman is for rank order. Cross-tabs are for categories, grouped statistics for categories against numbers. And three warnings: correlation is not causation, look at the scatterplot, and check subgroups for Simpson's paradox. If a question asks which tool you would use for a given pair of features, answer by naming the types first.
#3
Let me add the practical routine. Check the correlation matrix, compute the V I F for each feature, and decide whether any should go. Scale the features. Run K-Means for a few values of k, and look at both the silhouette score and how interpretable each result is. Name each cluster using group means. And remember the status of the result: it is exploratory, it describes the data you have, and it is not a model to predict new rows. Examiners often ask this last point.
#4
Let me connect the visualization ideas. Anscombe's quartet is the reason to plot. The grammar of graphics is the way to build and debug a chart. Facets are the tool for comparing groups. A truncated bar axis, dual axes, three-D effects and clutter are the main ways charts mislead. Treemaps, word clouds and networks handle non-numeric data. And a presentation needs a story with context, conflict and resolution, with insight titles on each slide.

Let me explain the first two answers further. For profit, the mean is positive because a minority of big orders are very profitable, but the median order loses one hundred fifty dollars. So you would report the median and the share of loss-making orders, and show the histogram. For Pearson against Spearman, the lesson is not that one is better, but that a large gap is a signal to look at a scatterplot, because outliers or a non-linear pattern are involved.
#7
Let me explain the remaining answers further. A V I F of twelve means the feature can be predicted almost perfectly from the others, so it adds little and destabilises models. You may drop it, combine it with related features, or use principal component analysis, which you will meet in module four. For the choice of k, a good answer shows judgement: state the silhouette score, state what each cluster would mean, and justify your pick. For the truncated bars, the answer is that length encodes the value, so cutting the baseline changes the message.
