#0
Let me tell you what to expect. This video is a complete worked example, so you can see how all the steps of practicals one and two fit together on one real dataset. The California housing data comes from the nineteen ninety census, and each row is a block group, which we call a district. It is a very popular dataset for teaching because it has clear skew, strong outliers and an obvious business question. I suggest you watch it once, and then repeat the same steps on a dataset of your own.
#1
Let me explain why the question is phrased this way. A lot of students compute statistics and draw plots without ever comparing them. Here we do both on purpose, so that we can check whether the story told by the numbers is the same story told by the pictures. If they disagree, one of them is misleading you, and finding out which is a useful skill. Notice also that the plan starts with cleaning and ends with comparison, which is exactly the routine from the lab video.
#2
Let me explain the cleaning logic. Duplicates are checked first, because a repeated row counts twice in every statistic. Two hundred seven missing values in total bedrooms are about one percent of the data, so dropping those rows is acceptable here, though in other cases you might impute instead, which you will learn in module three. Then we look for zeros in columns where zero is impossible. A district cannot have zero households, so a zero would be a recording error. These checks take two minutes in pandas using dot duplicated, dot isna and a simple comparison, and they protect everything that follows.
#3
Let me explain why naming variables matters here. If you plot house value against income, you are implicitly saying income is the explainer. Rooms, population and households all describe the size of a district, so they carry overlapping information. Later in the course you will see this problem called multicollinearity, and you will learn how to detect it. For now, remember the practical lesson: before you add many similar variables to a model, check how strongly they move together. And treat ocean proximity as a category, which we use to divide the data into groups.
#4
Let me explain these numbers. A skewness of one point six four is a clear right skew, because it is beyond one. A kurtosis of four point nine four is above zero, which means the tails are heavier than a normal curve, so extreme incomes are more common than a bell curve would predict. The mean is about nine point five percent above the median, which is the same signal in a simpler form. And six hundred seventy outliers, about three percent of districts, are all on the high side. These are wealthy neighbourhoods, which are real and not mistakes, so we keep them but remember them when we choose a summary.
#5
Let me explain why we reuse the code. Writing a function that draws the three plots for any column makes your work faster and less error-prone. You pass the column name, and you get the same layout every time, so differences between features are real differences and not differences in plotting style. A tip: use sharex so that the histogram, boxplot and density curve line up on the same axis. Then your eye can connect a tall bar in the histogram with the dots in the boxplot above it.
#6
Let me explain what this tells us. Near-zero skewness means the left and right sides are balanced, and the small negative kurtosis means the distribution is a little flatter than the bell curve, so houses of all ages are fairly evenly represented. There are no outliers, so the mean and the median are interchangeable here. This is the kind of column where reporting a mean and standard deviation is perfectly fine. It also reminds us that not every variable needs special treatment. Part of good exploration is noticing which columns are already well behaved.
#7
Let me explain this. When the total number of rooms in a district is mostly a few thousand but a few districts have tens of thousands, you get a long thin tail. A kurtosis of thirty two means that the tail carries a very large share of the variance. Twelve hundred ninety outliers might look alarming, but they are not mistakes. They are large districts. So the right response is not to delete them, but to think about transformation, for example with a logarithm, which we will study in module four, or to use per-household ratios instead of raw totals.
#8
Let me add a comment. When skewness is almost five and kurtosis is seventy four, the average is a poor description of a typical district. The median population is a much better one. And any model that is sensitive to extreme values, such as linear regression, can be pulled around by the dense urban districts. Notice that the same story shows up in rooms and population, which makes sense, because bigger districts have more rooms and more people. That repeated pattern is a hint that these features overlap strongly.
#9
Let me explain what this implies. A mean that is fifteen percent above the median and a skewness near one tell us the price distribution leans to the right, but not as sharply as population. The long tail beyond four hundred eighty thousand dollars is partly an artefact of the data: the census caps house values at five hundred thousand, so many expensive districts pile up at the cap. If you build a model on this data, remember that values at the cap are not true values. That is exactly the kind of detail a careful analyst notices, and it is only visible because we looked at the histogram.
#10
Let me read the table as a story. The size variables, rooms and population, are extreme because they depend on how big each district happens to be. House age is calm because it is a different kind of quantity, an age and not a count. Income and house value sit in between. This kind of comparison across columns is the real value of a summary table. It tells you where to spend your attention during cleaning, and which features will need a transformation before modelling.
#11
Let me explain how to read this table. A pivot table here has ocean proximity on the rows and the average house value in the cells. Inland districts are cheapest, which matches common sense, since coastal land is more desirable. The island category is a warning about small samples. With only five districts, one expensive neighbourhood would change the average completely. So always print the count alongside the mean, and be careful about drawing conclusions from groups with very few rows.
#12
Let me explain what additive means here. If location and income were interacting in a complicated way, the lines for each ocean category would cross or fan out. Instead, they rise in a roughly parallel way: every location category gets more expensive as income goes up, and the location sets the starting level. That suggests that a simple model with both income and location as inputs will work reasonably well, and it is the kind of insight that comes from a pivot table at almost no cost.
#13
Let me explain why the agreement matters. Suppose the table had said skewness near zero but the plot clearly showed two humps. That would tell you that the statistic is hiding something, such as two groups mixed together. In this dataset that does not happen, which is reassuring, but you only know it because you checked. Make this a rule for your own assignments: every number you report should have a plot next to it, and you should write one sentence saying whether they agree.
