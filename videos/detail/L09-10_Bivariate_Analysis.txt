#0
Let me explain the idea of this lecture in one line. Until now we studied columns one at a time, like looking at each ingredient on its own. Now we study pairs, like how two ingredients taste together. Most of the useful questions in data science are about pairs: does discount affect profit, do students who attend more score more, does the segment change the shipping choice. By the end you will be able to choose the right tool for each pair, read it correctly, and avoid the classic traps.
#1
Let me add how to decide quickly. Ask what type each of the two columns is. If both are numbers, think correlation and scatterplot. If both are categories, think cross-tab. If one is a category and one is a number, think grouped statistics and boxplots. This three-way split is the same as the one in the cheat sheet near the end of this video, and it saves you from the common mistake of computing a correlation between two categories, or a mean of a code.
#2
Let me give two examples. In the Superstore data, the average discount looks small and harmless. But when we plot discount against profit, we see that orders with large discounts tend to lose money, which suggests that discounting too much eats the margin. Another example: the average exam score tells you nothing about whether tutoring helps. Only comparing scores of students with and without tutoring answers that. So each interesting question is a pair, and each pair is a bivariate analysis.
#3
Let me explain the formula in words. For each row, take how far X is above or below its own average, and how far Y is above or below its own average, and multiply the two. If both are above their averages, or both below, the product is positive. If one is above and the other below, it is negative. Average all those products. A big positive average means they tend to rise together. The problem is that if you measure sales in rupees instead of dollars, the covariance changes, even though the relationship is the same, which is why we need correlation.
#4
Let me explain with examples. A correlation of zero point nine between height and weight is strong and positive. A correlation of minus zero point one between shoe size and exam score is close to zero. And a correlation of zero point four between hours studied and marks is moderate. Remember that r measures only straight-line association. A perfect parabola can have r of zero, even though one variable completely determines the other. That is why we always add a scatterplot, and why the next slides show a gallery of shapes.
#5
Let me give a famous example. Ice cream sales and drowning incidents are positively correlated across months. Ice cream does not cause drowning. Hot weather increases both, because people eat more ice cream and swim more. The hidden third factor is called a confounder. In business data, discount and sales volume may rise together because of a festival season, and not because of the discount. To argue for cause, you need an experiment or at least careful control of other factors. Correlation is a good start for a hypothesis, not an ending.
#6
Let me explain how ranking works. Spearman replaces every value with its rank, so the smallest becomes one, the next two, and so on, and then computes Pearson on the ranks. This means that only the order matters, and not how far apart the values are, so extreme outliers lose their power. In pandas you write df dot corr with method equals spearman. The big difference between minus zero point two two and minus zero point five four tells you that the relationship is consistently negative, but that a few extreme points were hiding it from Pearson.
#7
Let me explain how to draw it. In seaborn, sns dot scatterplot with x, y and the data frame gives the picture. With thousands of points, they overlap and hide the density, so reduce the marker size, add transparency with alpha, or use a hexbin or two-dimensional histogram. Adding a regression line with sns dot regplot shows the overall trend. Always label the axes with units. And look for groups of points that do not follow the main cloud, because those often reveal data problems or subgroups worth studying.
#8
Let me say what each pattern means. In the curvilinear case, a straight line misses the relationship, so r understates it. In the fanning pattern, the spread grows with x, which affects regression assumptions, and often suggests a transformation such as a logarithm. In the outlier case, one or two points create a correlation that disappears if you remove them. Anscombe's quartet is the famous demonstration, with four datasets that have the same mean, variance and correlation but completely different pictures. It is a good reason never to rely on a single number.
#9
Let me explain how to read a heatmap well. Use a diverging colour scale centred on zero, so that positive and negative are clearly different colours and zero is neutral. Print the values inside the cells with annot equals True. Look first for strong pairs, which tell you about redundant features, and then for features that correlate with your target. Because the matrix is symmetric, you can show only half of it with a mask, which makes it cleaner. In pandas the whole matrix is one call to df dot corr.
#10
Let me explain how to make one. In pandas, pd dot crosstab with the two columns gives the counts. Adding normalize equals index turns each row into percentages that add to one hundred. That is the form I recommend, because it answers the question: among customers in each segment, what share chooses each shipping mode? Add margins equals True to get totals. A stacked bar chart or a heatmap of the percentage table makes the pattern visible at a glance.
#11
Let me explain the idea behind this. If two categorical features were completely unrelated, each row of the percentage table would look like the overall split. The further the rows differ from each other, the stronger the association. A chi-square test turns this comparison into a number and a p-value, and you will meet it later. For now, a visual comparison is enough. Also check the counts behind the percentages, since fifty percent of four rows means little, but fifty percent of four thousand rows means a lot.
#12
Let me explain each tool in code. A grouped summary is df dot groupby of the category, then the column, then dot agg with mean, median and std. Boxplots by category are sns dot boxplot with x as the category and y as the number. A bar chart of means is sns dot barplot. The boxplot gives the most information, because it shows the spread and outliers in each group. When groups are very skewed, as profit is, prefer the median over the mean.
#13
Let me explain how to use this table. When you open a new dataset, start by listing the columns and their types. Then for the question you want to answer, locate the pair type and pick the tool in that row. Always add the second view, a plot with the number or a number with the plot, and write one sentence about what you found. This habit means that every analysis in your report has a clear reason and a clear check.
#14
Let me give an example for Simpson's paradox. In a famous admission dataset from the University of California, Berkeley, the overall figures seemed to show that women were admitted less often. But within most individual departments, women were admitted at equal or higher rates. The paradox came from the fact that women applied more to the departments that were harder to get into. Overall trends can hide or even reverse the pattern inside the groups. So whenever a relationship matters, check it within the important subgroups too.
