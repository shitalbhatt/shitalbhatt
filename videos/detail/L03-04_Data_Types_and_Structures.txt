#0
Let me explain why this lecture matters. Before you plot or model, you must know what kind of thing each column is. The type decides which summary is meaningful, which chart makes sense, and which model can accept it. A common beginner error is to calculate the average of a postal code or a phone number, because it looks like a number. By the end of this video you will be able to look at any column and say its scale, whether it is counted or measured, how it is stored, and what pattern to look for if it is a time series.
#1
Let me give you a small map. Scales of measurement are about what the numbers mean. Discrete versus continuous is about how the numbers arise. Structured versus semi-structured is about how the data is stored. Text data is about how words become features. And time series is about order in time. Together they let you answer the first question in any project: what exactly is in this file? You can already see how each of these will return in the labs.
#2
Let me give you more examples. Nominal: a student's branch, a city, or the mode of travel to college. There is no order, so you cannot say that the metro is higher than the bus, only that they are different. Ordinal: a feedback form with poor, average, good and excellent. We know excellent beats good, but we cannot say the gap between poor and average equals the gap between good and excellent. This matters because averaging ordinal codes, for example calling average two and excellent four, pretends that the gaps are equal. The median or the percentage in each category is safer.
#3
Let me explain with temperature. Thirty degrees Celsius is not twice as hot as fifteen degrees, because zero degrees Celsius is just a chosen point and does not mean no heat. But in kelvin, where zero means no thermal energy, ratios work. Compare that with weight: sixty kilograms really is twice thirty kilograms. The practical test is simple: ask whether it makes sense to say twice as much. If yes, it is ratio. If no, but differences make sense, it is interval. Years behave like interval data too: two thousand twenty is not twice two thousand ten.
#4
Let me turn this into a memory aid. Think of a ladder. At the bottom, nominal lets you count and compare labels. One step up, ordinal adds order. One more step, interval adds equal differences, so you can add and subtract. At the top, ratio adds a true zero, so you can multiply and divide. Each step keeps everything from the step below. So any statistic that is valid for nominal data is also valid for ratio data, but not the other way around.
#5
Let me clear up a common confusion. A column of whole numbers is not always discrete in the sense we mean. Age in completed years looks like whole numbers, but age itself is continuous, and we rounded it. Likewise, a price in rupees is measured to the paisa and so is effectively continuous. The question to ask is how the value arises. If you counted things, like the number of defects, it is discrete. If you measured something with an instrument, it is continuous. This distinction decides which probability distributions and which charts are suitable, for example bar charts for discrete counts and histograms for continuous measurements.
#6
Let me give a practical picture. A student attendance register is structured: every student has the same columns, name, roll number and attendance. A collection of customer reviews from a website, stored as J S O N, is semi-structured: one review has a photo and a title, another has only text, a third has a reply from the seller. You can still read these records, but you must flatten them into a table before analysis. In Python, the pandas function json normalize does exactly that. Remember that semi-structured does not mean messy. It just means the schema is flexible.
#7
Let me walk through the layers with an example. Suppose you have ten thousand product reviews. That set is your corpus. One review is a document. If a review says the battery is great, breaking it into the words the, battery, is and great gives four tokens. After lowercasing and removing stopwords like the and is, the vocabulary may keep battery and great. Stemming cuts words to a crude root, so running becomes run, while lemmatisation uses a dictionary to find the proper base form, so better becomes good. Counting how often each word appears in each document gives a table of numbers, which you can analyse like any other table.
#8
Let me give examples you can picture. Think of monthly sales of an ice cream shop. If every year the sales are a little higher than the year before, that is the trend. If every summer the sales jump and every winter they fall, that is seasonality, and it repeats at a fixed interval of twelve months. To see the trend, use a moving average, which smooths away the short-term wiggles. To see the seasonality, compare the same month across years. A common error is to compare December with November, find a jump, and call it growth, when it is just the usual seasonal pattern.
#9
Let me finish with a quick self-test. Take any dataset and for every column write four things: its scale, whether it is discrete or continuous, how it is stored, and whether it carries time. This small table, which we sometimes call a data dictionary, takes ten minutes and saves hours later.
