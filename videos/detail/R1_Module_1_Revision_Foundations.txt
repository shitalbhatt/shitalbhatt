Let me tell you how to use this revision video. Do not just listen. After each slide, pause the video and try to say the key points aloud in your own words, and only then listen to the summary. That is a much stronger way to learn than reading notes again. If you find a point you cannot explain, go back to that lecture video, and then do its lab once more. At the end, answer the five questions without looking at the answers.
#1
Let me connect these ideas into one story. You start by being honest about where the data came from, because a biased sample cannot be rescued. Then you describe it by drawing it first and summarising second, so you know whether the mean or the median is the right centre. You use the one point five interquartile range fence to flag outliers, but you investigate before deleting. If a friend asks what you learned in the first week, these three sentences are enough.
#2
Let me add memory hooks. For scales, remember the order nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio, or the ladder of what you can do: label, rank, subtract, divide. For counting versus measuring, remember that discrete is counted and continuous is measured. For storage, remember that structured has a fixed table and semi-structured has tags. For text, remember the chain corpus, document, token, term. For time series, ask for trend and seasonality, and separate them before drawing conclusions.
#3
Let me add some hooks again. An image is a grid of numbers, so you can take a histogram of its pixels. Audio is a one-dimensional list, so you can plot its waveform. The curse of dimensionality can be remembered as every new column multiplies the space, and the same number of rows covers less of it. For bias, remember that it enters at collection and labelling, so always split by group and count the rows, and finish your report with what the data cannot tell us.
#4
Let me suggest how to use the labs for revision. Choose one dataset, and repeat the labs in order. First, write a problem statement and run the five loading checks. Second, make the data dictionary of scales and types. Third, compute the mean and median, standard deviation and interquartile range, and plot histogram, boxplot and density. Fourth, add one pivot table. If you can do this on a new dataset within an hour, you have understood module one.
#5
Let me explain the first two answers in more detail. A mean of ninety five against a median of thirty nine is a very large gap, which means a few very large values are dragging the mean up. We would lead with the median and the interquartile range, and show the histogram so that the reader can see the tail. For the second, imagine a poll of ten million people that only includes those who own a smartphone. It is precise, but it is precisely wrong about people without smartphones. More rows of the same flawed design do not fix that.
#6
Let me explain the other answers too. Celsius is interval because zero degrees is a chosen point and not the absence of temperature. Features must be scaled before principal component analysis or K-Means because both depend on distances and variances. If one feature is in lakhs of rupees and another is in years, the first completely dominates, and the result reflects units and not structure. And a point far outside the fence may be a mistake, a rare real value, or a member of a different population, so we investigate before we delete.
