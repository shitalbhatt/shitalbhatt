We use one dataset throughout so that you can watch the same column through several different lenses. You will find the Superstore notebook in the lab folder. #1
Let me say why we chose this dataset. Titanic and Iris are useful, but everyone has seen them, and their results are well known, so you stop thinking. Superstore data is closer to what you will meet in a job: orders, discounts, losses and shipping. The key habit for the whole lecture is a three-step loop. Compute the numbers, draw the picture, and then ask whether the two agree. If they do not agree, you have found something interesting, and that is the best part of analysis.
#2
Let me explain how to think about each numeric feature. Sales is the order value, so it can only be positive. Profit can be negative, which already tells you that some orders lose money, and that fact will matter for the whole course. Discount is a rate, so it is bounded. Order quantity is a count. Unit price and shipping cost are amounts. Notice that I describe each feature with its meaning, its possible range, and a business sense of what a large or negative value would mean. Do this for every dataset you meet.
#3
Let me explain why the categorical features matter. They define the groups into which we will later split the numeric features. For example, does profit differ by product category, or does shipping cost differ by ship mode? Each categorical feature is a lens that divides the orders into a few baskets. We will study how the baskets compare in lecture eight. For now, notice that all three have a small number of levels, which makes them easy to summarise.
#4
Let me add a small detail. isna returns true or false for each cell, and sum counts the trues in each column, because true counts as one. You can add a divide by the length of the data frame and multiply by one hundred to get percentages. Also look for hidden missing values, such as the text na, a dash, a zero where zero is impossible, or minus one. Those are not caught by isna, and they need a separate check with value counts or a comparison.
#5
Let me explain what we do about the sixty three missing base margins. They are less than one percent, and base margin is not one of our six main features, so we simply note it. If it were an important feature, we would investigate why it is missing, for example whether it is missing for one product category, before deciding whether to drop or fill the values. That decision is part of module three, where we treat missing values seriously.
#6
Let me give you the pandas lines. For the mean and median, call df dot mean and df dot median with numeric only equal to True. For the mode, call df dot mode, which returns the most frequent values, and take the first row. Putting them into a single table with pd dot concat makes it easy to compare. A useful habit is to add a column that is the ratio of mean to median, so you can see at a glance which features are pulled by extreme values.
#7
Let me explain why this is so striking. Profit has a positive average of one hundred eighty one dollars, so a manager reading only the mean would say the business is healthy. But the typical order, the median one, loses a hundred fifty dollars. Both statements are correct, and they describe different things. The mean includes the very large profitable orders. The median describes the middle order. This is the exact reason we compare the two, and why reporting only one of them can mislead people.
#8
Let me give the code. Standard deviation is df dot std, range is df dot max minus df dot min, and the interquartile range is the difference between dot quantile of point seven five and dot quantile of point two five. A tip: compute all three in one small table with one row per feature, and then add a column that divides the standard deviation by the interquartile range. For a roughly normal feature this ratio is close to three quarters. A much larger ratio signals a heavy tail.
#9
Let me interpret these numbers. When the standard deviation is more than twice the interquartile range, a small number of huge values are inflating the spread. For sales, ordinary orders are tightly packed, with a middle half spanning about sixteen hundred dollars, while a few very large orders push the standard deviation above thirty five hundred. The same happens for profit, in both directions. For order quantity, the two spreads are in a sensible ratio, which is what a well-behaved column looks like.
#10
Let me explain how to read the two numbers together. A value of skewness above one or below minus one is a strong skew. Excess kurtosis near zero is like a bell curve, and anything above about three suggests heavy tails. Negative excess kurtosis means flat tails, like a uniform distribution. In pandas, df dot skew and df dot kurt give both in one line each. Always remember that these are sensitive to outliers themselves, so a very large value for either is a signal to look at the plot.
#11
Let me say what these values mean for your work. A kurtosis of two hundred seventy one for unit price is not a small oddity. It means that a handful of extremely expensive items dominate the fourth power in the formula, and the distribution is almost entirely a spike at low prices with a very long thin tail. Whenever you see numbers this large, plan to apply a transformation, such as a logarithm, before using the feature in any model. We will do this in module four.
