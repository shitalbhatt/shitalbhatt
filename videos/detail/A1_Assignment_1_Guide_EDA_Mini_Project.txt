Let me start with an important note. This guide is general advice, and your assignment brief is the final authority on deadlines, group size, dataset rules and marking. If anything I say here differs from the brief, follow the brief. What I can do is show you how a strong submission is usually built, using what you learned in the first two modules. Watch this video once before you start, and again when you are about to submit.
#1
Let me explain what the marking is looking for. Outcome one is about showing that you understand the dataset: its types, distributions, quality, and structure. Outcome two is about using the right exploratory technique for each situation: one variable, two variables, or many. Since this is a presentation, the quality of your explanation also matters. A correct analysis that nobody can follow earns fewer marks than a clear one. So plan time for both the analysis and the story.
#2
Let me give a suggested time plan. In the first week, choose the dataset and write the problem statement. In the second, do the audit and the univariate analysis. In the third, do the bivariate and multivariate steps and write your hypotheses. In the fourth, build the story and rehearse. Each stage corresponds to a lecture and lab you have already seen, so use the videos as a reference for the code. Starting early is the best way to avoid last-minute mistakes.
#3
Let me explain how to choose a good dataset. A good dataset has at least a few thousand rows, ten or more columns, with both numbers and categories, and it should have some mess, such as missing values or skew, because that gives you something to explore. Sources such as Kaggle, the UCI repository, data dot gov dot in, and open government portals are good starting points. Avoid datasets that are too small, too clean, or already used in class. Read the licence, and note the source in your report.
#4
Let me add some practical details. For every numeric column, give the mean, median, standard deviation, interquartile range, skewness and the number of outliers, and a histogram or box plot. For every pair that you claim is related, show a scatterplot and the correlation, using Spearman if the data is skewed. For categories, show frequency tables and percentages. When you remove or fix something, say exactly what and why. Write every finding as a sentence a non-technical manager could understand.
#5
Let me give an example of a slide title. Instead of average profit by region, write the Central region averages a loss while every other region makes a profit. Use a highlighted colour for the key bar and grey for the rest. Keep the slide uncluttered, with one chart and one or two lines of text. In the spoken part, say what the chart shows, why it matters and what you recommend. Practise with a timer so that you finish within the allotted time.
#6
Let me explain how to use the checklist. Go through it a day before submission, and tick each item honestly. If you find a gap, such as an unlabelled axis, fix it. The last question, can you explain every number, is the one examiners test most. Be ready for questions like why did you use the median here, why did you remove this row, or what does this correlation tell us. If you can answer these from your notes, you are well prepared.
#7
Let me remind you of the main points. Be honest about what you did to the data, show a plot with every statistic, and avoid claims beyond what your sample supports. Keep your notebook clean and runnable from top to bottom, with short markdown notes explaining each step. Submit on time, and keep a backup copy. If you are stuck, use the lecture and lab videos, and ask your instructor early, not on the last day. Good luck, and I look forward to your presentations.
