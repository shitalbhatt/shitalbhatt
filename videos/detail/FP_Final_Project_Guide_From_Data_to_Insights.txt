#0
Let me start with the usual advice. Your instructor's brief and rubric decide the dates, the group size, the dataset rules and the marking. This guide explains how to turn what you learned into a project that scores well and also reads like real analytical work. Watch it once now, to plan, and once again a week before the deadline, to check yourself. The project is thirty percent of the course, so it deserves careful planning, and the time you spend on the question and the structure pays back many times.
#1
Let me explain what summative means here. The assignments checked parts of the course. The final project checks that you can carry out the whole process alone, from a raw table to a recommendation. The marker will look for evidence of all four outcomes. So a project that only draws charts, or only builds a model, will lose marks. You need a balance: understanding the data, exploring it, preparing it, and explaining it. Choose a dataset where each of these has something to do.
#2
Let me explain how to choose. A good question is narrow and answerable, such as which factors are associated with customers leaving, or which patient features predict a disease. A poor question is vague, such as analyse this dataset. Sources such as Kaggle, the UCI repository, and government open data portals offer many files, and you can find domain examples in insurance, retail, pharma and healthcare. Check the licence, and read the data dictionary. Finish your problem statement with the two-blank sentence about what your sample can and cannot support, because it protects you from overclaiming later.
#3
Let me explain why this structure helps you. Each part answers a question that a reviewer would ask. What is this about? What are you trying to find out and how will you judge success? What do you believe, and how will you test it? Is the data any good? What does it show? How do you know the model works? What should we do? And what are the limits? If you write the headings first, and fill them in as you go, you will never be unsure what is missing. Keep the notebook tidy enough that a stranger could follow it.
#4
Let me give an example. If you notice from the plots that discount seems linked to loss, that claim is exploratory, because it came from looking. To confirm it, you would test it on rows you set aside before looking, or on new data, and say so. If you run twenty tests, one will probably look significant by chance, so apply a correction or mention the issue. A short table with the headline claims, labelled exploratory or confirmatory, makes your project look careful, and it is easy to produce.
#5
Let me explain the order. Audit and log the problems first. Then explain missing values before filling them, and compare at least two methods. Treat outliers according to their cause, and record how many rows were affected. Encode and transform according to the type of each column. Consider features and dimension reduction only if they help, and test them with the same model and split. Put anything that learns from the data, such as scalers, imputers and selectors, in a pipeline, fitted on the training rows only. This is the single most common source of marks lost, so check it twice.
#6
Let me give an example of an insight. Observation: customers on month-to-month contracts leave more often. Evidence: thirty percent against five percent for those on two-year contracts. Meaning: the lack of commitment, or the lack of loyalty benefits. Action: offer an incentive to switch to a longer contract. Notice how each part is short and concrete. On your slides, put the insight in the title, show one chart that proves it, and say the action aloud. Practise with a friend who does not know the data, and ask whether they understood.
#7
Let me explain how to use the checklist. Go through it a few days before submission, so there is time to fix gaps. The question about why you chose each method is the one markers ask most often, so prepare a one-line reason for every step. And rerun the notebook from a fresh kernel, because errors often appear only then. If anything is missing, fix the important things first, such as the leakage and the clarity of your conclusion, before polishing the formatting.
#8
Let me end with a few words. A focused project, with one clear question, an honest method and a clear recommendation, is better than a big project with many unfinished ideas. Start early, keep a log as you work, and ask for feedback while there is still time to act on it. Remember that the point of the course was to teach you to look before you conclude, and to report what you found honestly. Show that in your project, and the marks will follow. Good luck.
