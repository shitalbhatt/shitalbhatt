#0
Let me explain what is new in this lecture. Pairs are not enough, because the real world has many features that interact. Two ideas help us here. The first is about columns: are some features redundant, so that they say the same thing in different words? The second is about rows: do the records fall into natural groups? The first leads to the variance inflation factor, and the second to clustering with K-Means. Both are tools for understanding structure before you build a model.
#1
Let me give an example for each. Redundant features: house area in square feet and house area in square metres are the same information. Less obviously, total rooms, bedrooms, and households in the California data move together because all measure district size. Hidden groups: in a shop's orders, there may be small everyday purchases, large bulk orders, and heavily discounted clearance orders. No single column shows these three groups, but a combination of columns does.
#2
Let me explain the practical consequences. In a linear regression, if two features are nearly copies, the model cannot decide which one gets the credit, so the coefficients become unstable, and may even change sign when you add a few rows. In distance-based methods like K-Means, a repeated feature counts twice in the distance, so it gets double the weight without anyone deciding that. And when you explain results to others, you cannot say that this feature matters, when it only matters together with its twin.
#3
Let me work out the number. Suppose feature A is predicted from the others with an R squared of zero point eight. Then one minus R squared is point two, and one divided by point two is five. So the V I F is five, which is at the border of worth investigating. If R squared is zero point nine, the V I F is ten, which is severe. Think of the V I F as telling you how many times larger the variance of the coefficient is, compared with a world where the feature was independent. In Python, statsmodels offers variance inflation factor, which you call for each column of the feature matrix.
#4
Let me explain the difference in a school example. If you have the marks of each student in several subjects and also their final grade, supervised learning would predict the grade from the marks. Clustering instead looks only at the marks and asks: are there natural types of students, such as strong in maths but weak in languages, or the reverse? Nobody told the algorithm that these types exist. This is useful for exploration, for customer segments, and for finding unusual groups.
#5
Let me stress the word exploratory. When we run K-Means in this course, we are not promising that the clusters are real groups in nature. We are asking whether the data, when simplified into a few groups, gives us a useful summary. If the groups are interpretable and stable, they may suggest segments to study further. If not, we learn that the data has no strong group structure. Both are useful results, and neither involves predicting anything for new data.
#6
Let me walk through a tiny example. Suppose you have eight points on a line, and you ask for two clusters. You pick two random starting centres. Every point goes to its nearest centre, forming two groups. You move each centre to the average of its group. Some points may now be closer to the other centre, so they switch. You repeat until nobody switches, which usually takes only a few rounds. A caution is that the result depends on the starting centres, so scikit-learn runs the algorithm several times with different starts and keeps the best one, controlled by the n init setting.
#7
Let me give an example with numbers. Imagine two features, sales in dollars and discount as a fraction. The distance between two orders is dominated by the difference in sales, because a difference of five hundred dollars is enormous compared with a difference of point two in discount. The algorithm effectively ignores discount. After standardising, a difference of one standard deviation in sales is comparable to one standard deviation in discount. In scikit-learn, you fit StandardScaler on the data, transform it, and then run KMeans on the transformed data.
#8
Let me explain the process for choosing k. You run K-Means for k equal to two, three, four and so on, and compute the silhouette score for each, using silhouette score from scikit-learn. You plot the scores and look for the peak. But do not stop at the number. Look at the clusters for each of the top candidates, and ask which one is easiest to explain and most useful. Here k equals two only separates small orders from large ones, which we could have guessed. Three gives a richer story, so the practical choice is three.
#9
Let me explain how to read these plots. Choose two or three features that matter and draw a scatterplot, with colour by cluster. If the colours form clear regions, the clusters correspond to something visible. If they are mixed like a salad, the clusters are weak. In the pairplot, look at which pairs of features separate the clusters best, because those are the features that define the groups. Be careful that a two-dimensional plot is only a shadow of a higher-dimensional space, so clusters that look overlapping may be separated in another dimension.
#10
Let me read these clusters like a business analyst. The big group of about sixty nine hundred orders is ordinary, so there is little to say. The high-value group is small but important, because those orders have large sales and good profit, and a company would want to protect those customers. The surprising group is cluster one, with sixty-six percent discount and a loss of about one hundred seven dollars on average. If this group is real, it suggests that a policy of deep discounts is hurting the business. We would verify this by examining those orders, not by trusting the algorithm alone.
#11
Let me explain the last caution with an example. The algorithm will give you k clusters even if the data is one uniform cloud, because it always divides the space. So a result of three clusters is not proof that three groups exist. Check stability by running with different random seeds and on different subsets, and check that the clusters differ on features you did not use for clustering. Remember also the earlier lectures. Outliers can drag cluster centres, so look at them before clustering.
#12
Let me close with a practice plan. Take a dataset with at least five numeric features. Compute the V I F of each feature, and if any is above five, decide which feature to drop and why. Scale the rest, run K-Means for k from two to six, and plot the silhouette scores. Choose a k with a reason, profile each cluster with grouped means, and write one sentence on what each cluster is. Then write one hypothesis to test. The lab does all this in code. Thank you for watching.
