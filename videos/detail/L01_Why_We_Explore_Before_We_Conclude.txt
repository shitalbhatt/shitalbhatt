This course is built so that every lecture is followed by a lab, and everything we say here you will practise there. We begin with a simple belief that good data scientists share: most of the real work in a project is understanding and preparing the data, not fitting the model. If the foundation is wrong, no clever algorithm can rescue the result.
#1
Let me give you the map in plain words. Idea one asks where exploration sits in a project. Idea two separates two jobs that students often mix up: exploring to find questions, and confirming to test a claim. Idea three asks whether your sample can even speak for the people you care about. Then in the second lecture we move to numbers: the mean and median, how a distribution is shaped, and why robust statistics survive outliers. #2
Let me add some background. John Tukey was a statistician at Princeton and Bell Labs, and his book Exploratory Data Analysis, published in nineteen seventy seven, changed how people work with data. He wanted analysts to stop rushing to tests and start by looking. He used the word resistant for summaries that do not jump around when a few extreme values appear, and we will meet those later. One of his best known lines says that the greatest value of a picture is when it forces us to notice what we never expected to see. Think of a doctor taking your history and examining you before ordering any test. That is exploration. The test comes later.
#3
Here is what that looks like at each stage. When you collect data, you explore to check how many responses came from each college or each city, so you notice early that one group is missing. When you clean, you plot every column to spot impossible values. When you model, you look at residual plots to see where the model fails. When you evaluate, you break the errors down by subgroup, because an overall score of ninety percent can hide a group where the model is wrong half the time. Notice that each of these is a question answered by looking, and each ends in a decision you can write down and defend.
#4
Let me make the five jobs concrete using a simple table of student marks. Sanity: do the marks lie between zero and one hundred, and do the number of rows match the number of students? Quality: are there blanks, repeated roll numbers, or a mark of nine hundred typed by mistake? Structure: do marks rise with attendance, do some branches cluster together, is there a drop every Monday exam? Hypotheses: maybe late joiners score lower, to be tested later on fresh data. Modelling decisions: marks are skewed, so perhaps a transformation helps, and a simple baseline of predicting the class average tells us what any model must beat.
#5
Let me explain this example properly because it is famous in teaching. The Pima Indians diabetes dataset has seven hundred sixty eight records and eight measurements per patient, and it was collected by the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases. If you count the zeros, glucose has five, blood pressure has thirty five, skin thickness has two hundred twenty seven, insulin has three hundred seventy four, and body mass index has eleven. A living patient cannot have a blood pressure of zero, so these zeros are missing values wearing a disguise. In Python, one line does it: df of the suspect columns, compare equal to zero, then sum. The lesson is simple. Always read the minimum and maximum of every column before you trust anything.
#6
Let me add one example. Suppose you look at student data and notice that students who sit in the front row score higher. Exploring this is perfectly healthy. It gives you a candidate idea. But if you then test the same idea on the same rows and report a small p-value, you have fooled yourself, because the data suggested the question and the same data answered it. Statisticians Andrew Gelman and Eric Loken call this the garden of forking paths: with many choices along the way, almost any dataset will show something that looks significant. Do twenty independent tests at five percent and the chance of at least one false alarm is about sixty four percent. So explore freely, but confirm only on data the exploration never touched.
#7
Let me explain each habit. Splitting means keeping, say, twenty percent of your rows in a locked drawer and never plotting them until your idea is final. Writing the hypothesis down before testing means the date and wording are fixed, so you cannot quietly change the question to fit the answer. Labelling claims helps your reader: this pattern is exploratory, this one was confirmed on held-out data. For multiple testing, the simplest fix is the Bonferroni correction, where you divide your significance level by the number of tests. A gentler method, from Benjamini and Hochberg, controls the false discovery rate. You do not need the formulas today, only the habit of asking how many tests you ran.
#8
Let me give you an example from our own campus. Imagine you want to know how satisfied students are with placement support at your university. Your target population is every student. Your frame is the people on the class WhatsApp group, since that is where you can send the form. Your sample is the students who actually filled it. Your estimate is the average rating. Look at the two gaps. Students not on the group are left out, which is coverage error. Students on the group who ignored the form are non-response. Both gaps can push the estimate in a direction, and the number on your screen gives no warning at all.
#9
Let me give one example for each of the six. Selection bias: studying only students who attend class to judge how hard the course is. Self-selection: an online poll where people with strong views vote. Survivorship: studying only startups that still exist to learn what makes startups succeed. Non-response: a survey where busy people never reply. Undercoverage: a phone survey that reaches only people who own a landline. Measurement bias: a question worded to push people toward one answer, or a sensor that always reads two degrees high. Each one leaves you with a neat table that looks complete but quietly misrepresents the world.
#10
Let me tell you both stories with a little more detail. In nineteen thirty six, the magazine Literary Digest predicted that Alf Landon would beat Franklin Roosevelt, and Roosevelt won by a landslide. The magazine had about two point four million replies, a giant sample, but its lists came from car owners and telephone directories, which in the Depression meant wealthier voters. A young pollster, George Gallup, predicted the right winner with a much smaller but carefully designed sample. The second story is about Abraham Wald, a statistician at Columbia University during the war, who realised that the military should armour the places on returning planes where there were no bullet holes, because planes hit there never made it back. Both stories teach one question: who is missing from my data?
#11
Let me give you an everyday picture before the dartboard. Imagine a bathroom scale. If it always shows two kilograms extra, that is bias, because it is wrong in the same direction every time. If it shows a different number each time you step on it, with the average correct, that is variance, because it is unsteady. A good scale is neither. Most real measurements, and most real models, have a little of both, and part of your job is to find out which one is hurting you.
#12
Let me read the dartboard slowly. Top left, low bias and low variance: the darts are tight and on the bullseye, which is what we want. Bottom left, high bias with low variance: the darts are tight but in the wrong place, consistent and wrong. Top right, low bias with high variance: darts scatter around the bullseye, right on average, but any single throw may be far off. Bottom right, high bias and high variance, is the worst: scattered and also in the wrong place. In analysis, the bullseye is the true value in the population, and each dart is an estimate from one possible sample.
#13
Let me show you the arithmetic. The typical size of random error in a sample mean is the standard deviation divided by the square root of the sample size. If the standard deviation is ten and you have one hundred observations, the random error is about one. Move to four hundred observations and it falls to five tenths. So four times the data halves the noise, exactly as the slide says. Now think of the scale that reads two hundred grams heavy. Weigh a bag one thousand times and take the average: you still have two hundred grams of extra, because averaging cancels randomness but cannot cancel a consistent push in one direction.
#14
Let me read this chart for you. At one hundred observations the random error is large. At four hundred it has halved, at sixteen hundred it has halved again, and by ten thousand it is small but still falling slowly. Look at the flat line, though. That is the bias, and it does not budge at any sample size. This is the key warning. If you spend money collecting ten times more data from the same flawed process, you pay ten times more and the part of the error that matters most stays exactly where it was.
#15
Let me add the numbers. With fifty reviews and a standard deviation of one star, the margin of error on the average is about two tenths of a star, so three point nine and four point two are not very different after all. That is variance, and a bigger sample would settle it. Now compare a sample of two million, all from customers who were happy enough to reply. The margin of error is tiny, maybe a thousandth of a star, yet the average is still too high because unhappy customers are missing. The small margin makes the wrong answer look trustworthy, which is exactly why a big biased sample is more dangerous than a small honest one.
#16
Let me explain with a school example. Suppose you want to predict marks from hours studied and you have ten students. A straight line may miss the curve in the data, and that is underfitting: it is too simple, so it is biased. A wiggly curve of degree nine passes through all ten points exactly, which looks perfect on those students but jumps wildly for the eleventh student. That is overfitting, and it is like a student who memorises last year's paper word for word. He scores full marks on that paper but struggles when the questions change. The best model understands the pattern and ignores the noise.
#17
Let me explain each term. Bias squared is the gap between what your model would predict on average and the truth. Variance is how much predictions change when you retrain on a different sample. Irreducible noise is the randomness that nothing can predict. You reduce bias by giving the model more capacity, for example more features or a deeper tree. You reduce variance with regularisation, such as ridge or lasso, with pruning, or with more training rows. Noise is the floor. If you predict exam marks from hours studied, luck on the day, the questions asked and the student's health are noise that no model can remove.
#18
Let me explain how to find the bottom of the U. In k-fold cross-validation with k equal to five, you split the data into five equal parts. You train on four parts and test on the fifth, then repeat so that every part is used once for testing, and finally average the five scores. You do this for several model complexities, for example polynomial degrees one to ten, and pick the complexity with the lowest average test error. This is much more honest than judging a model on the same rows it learned from, because training error always keeps falling as the model becomes more flexible.
#19
Let me add numbers. Suppose training error is fourteen and test error is fifteen. Both are high and close together, so the model is too simple: high bias, and more data will barely help. Now suppose training error is two and test error is fifteen. The model has memorised the training rows, so the gap is the problem: high variance, and more data will help a lot. Plotting error against training size, called a learning curve, makes this visible. If the two curves meet at a high value, think bias. If there is a wide gap between them, think variance.
#20
Let me give an example. Suppose you build a model to predict whether students will complete a degree, using data only from students in metro cities. You can tune the model, cross-validate it and add rows, and its test score will look excellent. But when you use it on students from small towns, it may fail badly, because those students were never in your data. Every technique on this slide works inside the data you have. Deciding who gets into the data is a design decision you must make before collection, which is why we spent so long on sampling.
#21
Let me turn this into a short checklist you can write on a card. One: bias repeats, variance reshuffles. Two: more data cures variance, not bias. Three: in models, more flexibility lowers bias and raises variance. Four: in samples, only a better design fixes bias. If you can explain these four lines in your own words to a friend, you have understood the heart of this lecture.
