@video L01_Why_We_Explore_Before_We_Conclude
L:1 | Introduction | Welcome to Data two oh nine, Advanced Exploratory Data Analysis. In these first two lectures we build the foundation of the whole course: how to move from raw data to numbers you can actually trust. We start with why we explore data, then look at sampling, bias and variance.
L:2 | Roadmap | Over two lectures we cover six ideas. The role of E D A in the data science lifecycle, exploratory versus confirmatory analysis, and sampling bias. Then summary statistics, the shape of a distribution, and robust statistics like the median and the I Q R. This video covers the first three, plus the idea of bias and variance.
L:4 | The idea behind EDA | John Tukey introduced exploratory data analysis in nineteen seventy seven as its own discipline. The idea is to look at data with an open mind, using pictures and resistant summaries, so that the data can surprise you. Think of E D A as detective work, and confirmatory analysis as the trial that comes afterwards.
L:5 | Role of EDA in the lifecycle | E D A is not stage two of a project. It runs through the whole lifecycle. Every arrow going into the exploration band is a question you answer by looking at data, and every arrow coming out is a decision you can defend in your report. You explore when you collect, when you clean, when you model, and when you evaluate.
L:6 | What EDA buys you | Exploration gives you five concrete things. Sanity: does the file contain what the documentation claims? Quality: missing values, duplicates, impossible values. Structure: shapes, relationships, clusters and trends. Hypotheses worth testing later. And modelling decisions, such as transformations, features, and what a sensible baseline looks like.
L:7 | Worked example: Pima diabetes | Here is five minutes of looking. A quick describe call on seven hundred sixty eight patient records shows minimum values that are medically impossible: a glucose of zero, a blood pressure of zero. These were never missing in the file. Missingness had been encoded as zero. A model trained on this untouched would learn that no test predicts health. No algorithm tuning recovers from that, but five minutes of E D A does.
L:8 | Exploratory vs confirmatory | These are two different jobs with two different sets of rules. Exploratory analysis asks what is going on, allows unlimited looks, and produces candidate hypotheses. Confirmatory analysis asks whether one specific claim is supported, gets one pre-specified look, and produces p-values. The fatal mistake in exploration is refusing to look. The fatal mistake in confirmation is testing a hypothesis that the same data suggested.
L:9 | How professionals keep them apart | Professionals follow four habits. First, split the data and hold out a confirmation set before you plot anything. Second, write the hypothesis down before testing. Third, label every claim in the report as exploratory or confirmatory. Fourth, adjust for multiple testing when one dataset has to carry many tests. In this course, your final project must label each headline claim this way.
L:10 | Population, frame, sample, estimate | Four things people call the data are not the same. The target population is everyone the claim is about. The sampling frame is the list you could actually draw from. The sample is the rows you ended up with, and the estimate is the number you compute. The gap between population and frame is coverage error. The Online Shoppers data has no row for a person who never visited, so it says nothing about why they stayed away.
L:11 | Six ways samples go wrong | A sample can stop representing the population in six ways. Selection bias, self-selection, and survivorship bias in the top row. Non-response bias, undercoverage, and measurement bias in the bottom row. For example, product reviews come from delighted and furious customers, while the indifferent majority stays silent.
L:12 | Two cases worth remembering | Two cases to remember. In nineteen thirty six, the Literary Digest mailed ten million ballots and got over two million replies, yet predicted the wrong winner, because its address lists skewed wealthy. And in the Second World War, Abraham Wald pointed out that bullet holes on returning aircraft showed where planes could survive a hit. The missing rows were the planes that never came back. Always ask: what would a row look like that could never have been recorded?
L:13 | Bias and variance | Now a second big idea: two ways of being wrong. Bias is where you aim. Variance is how steady your hand is.
L:14 | The dartboard | One picture holds the whole idea. Low bias and low variance means tight darts on the bullseye. High bias means tight darts in the wrong place. High variance means darts scattered everywhere. The top right, high bias and high variance, is the worst place to be: wrong, and confident about it.
L:15 | Systematic vs random error | Bias is systematic error. You are off in the same direction every time, like a scale that reads two hundred grams heavy. It does not shrink with more data. Variance is random error. Two samples of fifty give two different means. It does shrink as data grows, roughly like one over the square root of n.
L:16 | More data cures only one | This chart shows error as the sample grows from one hundred to ten thousand. The random error curve falls, but slowly: four times the data only halves it. The bias line never moves. Past a point, collecting more data only shrinks the error you were not worried about.
L:17 | Sense one: in your data | In sampling, bias means the sample is systematically unlike the population, for example when only satisfied customers reply. Variance means the sample is fair but small, so fifty customers give three point nine stars and another fifty give four point two. A big biased sample is more dangerous than a small random one, because a narrow margin of error makes the wrong answer look precise.
L:18 | Sense two: in your model | The same words describe models. A model that is too simple underfits: high bias, low variance. A model that is too flexible overfits: it passes through every point, including the noise, so different data gives a wildly different model. The best model sits in between. Same ten data points; the only thing that changed is how much freedom the model had.
L:19 | Decomposing prediction error | Expected prediction error equals bias squared, plus variance, plus irreducible noise. Bias is fixed by adding capacity. Variance is fixed by removing freedom: regularisation, pruning, more training data. Noise cannot be fixed by any model. It sets the floor on how good you can get.
L:20 | The trade-off, drawn | Here is the trade-off. On the left, simple models, where bias dominates. On the right, flexible models, where variance dominates. The total error you actually pay is U-shaped, and cross-validation is how you find the bottom of that U.
L:21 | Diagnosis | How do you tell which one you have? High bias shows high training error and high test error that is close to it; more data barely helps. High variance shows very low training error and much higher test error; more data helps a lot. The quick test: does the gap between training and test error worry you more than the level of both?
L:22 | An important caution | One caution. The trade-off is about estimation, not collection. Regularisation, cross-validation and more training rows all work on error inside your model. None of them repairs a sample that never contained the people you care about. Sampling bias is fixed at the design stage, or not at all.
L:23 | Remember this much | Remember this: bias repeats, variance reshuffles. More data shrinks variance and leaves bias where it was. In models, complexity trades one for the other. In samples, only better design fixes bias.
L:24 | Recap and practice | To recap: E D A is a habit that recurs at every stage. Exploration generates hypotheses; confirmation tests them, never on the same rows. And sample size fixes noise, while only sample design fixes bias. Before the next class, load the Online Shoppers dataset and write the two-blank sentence: this sample can support claims about, blank, and cannot support claims about, blank.

@video L02_Distributions_Summaries_Robust_Statistics
L:25 | Introduction | In lecture two we look at shape before numbers: distributions, summary statistics, and robust statistics. Skewness, kurtosis, outliers, and the mean, median and I Q R.
L:26 | Why shape matters | The number hides the picture. Here are three distributions with the same kind of average. In the symmetric one the mean describes almost everybody. In the two-group one the mean lands in the gap and describes nobody. In the right-skewed one the mean sits above most of the bars. Same statistic, three realities. That is why we plot first and summarise second.
L:27 | The vocabulary | Every distribution answers three questions. Where is it: the centre, measured by mean, median or mode. How wide is it: the spread, measured by range, standard deviation or I Q R. And what shape is it: skewness, kurtosis and stray points.
L:28 | Skewness | Skewness tells you which way the tail points. Left-skewed data has a long tail on the left, like exam marks where most students score high. Symmetric data has both tails alike. Right-skewed data has a long tail on the right, like income, house prices and waiting times. The tail names the skew, not the hump.
L:29 | Skew pulls the mean | Skew pulls the mean but not the median. In right-skewed data, mode is less than median, which is less than mean. In left-skewed data the order reverses. This is why average income is higher than what a typical person earns: a few very large values sit in the tail and only the mean feels them. When the tail is long, report the median.
L:30 | Kurtosis | Kurtosis measures how often extreme values happen. It is about the weight in the tails, not the sharpness of the peak. Excess kurtosis is kurtosis minus three, so a normal curve scores zero. Above zero means heavy tails and more outliers expected. Below zero means light tails.
L:31 | Three kurtosis families | Platykurtic distributions have thin tails and extremes are genuinely rare. Mesokurtic is the bell curve benchmark. Leptokurtic distributions have fat tails: shocks and outliers are common, and standard deviation understates the risk. High kurtosis is a warning that your outlier check is about to find something.
L:32 | Outliers and the box plot | An outlier is a point far from the bulk of the data, and far needs a definition. The usual one is the one point five times I Q R fence. The I Q R is Q three minus Q one, the middle fifty percent. Points beyond Q one minus one point five I Q R, or Q three plus one point five I Q R, are flagged. Because the fence is built from quartiles, the outliers cannot move it. A flag is a question, not a verdict.
L:33 | Before you delete anything | There are three reasons a point sits far out. It may be a mistake, like an age of nine ninety nine; fix or remove it and say so. It may be rare but real, like a genuinely huge order; keep it, it is often the most interesting row. Or it may belong to the wrong population, like a wholesale buyer inside retail data; separate it and analyse the groups apart.
L:34 | The summary statistics toolkit | Here is the toolkit sorted by what each statistic tells you. For centre: mean, median and mode. For spread: range, standard deviation and I Q R. For shape: skewness and kurtosis. The last column shows when each breaks down. The mean is dragged by one extreme value, while the median and I Q R rarely break. Always report a centre and a spread together.
L:35 | Why robustness matters | Seven monthly salaries, in thousands: twenty eight up to forty two. Now the manager joins at four hundred. The mean more than doubles and describes nobody in the room, while the median moves by one unit. A statistic that one row can move this far is not a summary. It is a hostage.
L:36 | A working rule | So which do you report? If the histogram looks symmetric with no stray points, use mean and standard deviation. If the tail is long, use median and I Q R and say the data is skewed. If there are extreme points of unknown cause, report both and show how much they differ. And always put the picture next to the number.
L:37 | Remember this much | Plot first, summarise second. Skewness says which side the tail is on. Kurtosis says how heavy the tails are. The one point five I Q R fence flags candidates; investigate them, do not sweep them away. Mean and standard deviation for tidy symmetric data, median and I Q R for everything else.
L:38 | Six mistakes | Six mistakes cost marks and credibility. Deleting outliers silently. Reporting the mean of a skewed column. Using z greater than three on a small sample, where the ceiling is n minus one over root n. Treating a sentinel like zero, minus one or nine ninety nine as a number. Testing a hypothesis the same data suggested. And trusting one plot instead of at least two views.
L:39 | Takeaways | To take away: E D A is a habit through the whole lifecycle. Explore freely, confirm strictly, never on the same rows. A bigger sample shrinks noise; only a better sample removes bias. Summary statistics compress and compression loses structure, so plot it. Shape first, summary second. And median and I Q R survive contamination; mean, standard deviation and range do not. Next up is the lab on the Online Shoppers dataset.

LAB:1 | Introduction | Welcome to practicals one and two. Today we go from a vague question to a distribution. We frame the problem, load data, name our variables, describe the numbers, and visualise their shape.
LAB:2 | The path | The path has four stages. Frame and load: write a problem statement, then take a first look at the data. Name your variables: what you are explaining and what might explain it. Describe the numbers: mean versus median, variance versus I Q R, skewness. And see the shape with histograms, boxplots, density plots and pivot tables.
LAB:3 | Step 1: problem statement | Before you load a single row, put your question into words. What question am I answering? What is one row in my data? What outcome am I explaining? And what would an answer let me decide? For example: do customers who receive a discount email spend more per month than those who do not? A problem statement earns its place only if the answer changes an action.
LAB:4 | Step 2: load and look | Now load the data and confirm what actually arrived. Five steps: read the file with read C S V, check the shape, preview rows with head, check data types, and count missing values with isna dot sum. Do this every time, even for a dataset you have seen before, because column names, types and row counts change more often than you expect.
LAB:5 | Step 3: dependent vs independent | Next, decide what you are trying to explain and what might explain it. The dependent variable, Y, is the outcome, such as monthly spend, exam score or churn. The independent variables, X, are the factors that might influence it, such as a discount email or study hours. The quick test: if I built a chart with this on the y axis, would the rest of my analysis point at it?
LAB:6 | Mean vs median | Our running example is six hundred customers' monthly spend, which is right-skewed. The mean is thirty eight sixty three dollars; the median is thirty four fifty nine. A few big spenders drag the mean upward, while the median barely moves. Mean greater than median tells us the data is pulled right.
LAB:7 | Variance vs IQR | For spread we compare standard deviation, twenty dollars fifty five, with the I Q R, twenty three fifty. Standard deviation uses every value and is sensitive to outliers. The I Q R covers the middle fifty percent and is robust. Rule of thumb: if the data has outliers or skew, lead with the median and I Q R.
LAB:8 | Skewness | You can read the shape without plotting, just by comparing mean and median. Mean below median means a long left tail. Mean about equal to median means balanced. Mean above median means a long right tail.
LAB:9 | Three views | Plot the same distribution three ways. A histogram is best for spotting overall shape. A boxplot shows the median and I Q R and flags outliers. A density curve is a smoothed histogram, best for comparing shapes across groups. Each view highlights something different, so use more than one.
LAB:10 | Pivot tables | A pivot table answers: what is the average, total or count, broken down by category? You choose rows, columns, values and an aggregation. In this example, East sells more widgets than gadgets while South is the reverse, a pattern a single average would hide. In pandas it is one call to pivot table.
LAB:11 | Cheat sheet | Here is your cheat sheet. Question and unit of observation: problem statement. Explain versus explainer: dependent and independent variables. Is the typical value pulled by outliers: compare mean and median. Is the spread robust: compare standard deviation and I Q R. Which way does it lean: skewness. What does it look like: histogram, boxplot, density. And the summary by group: a pivot table. Try these on your own dataset. Thanks for watching.
