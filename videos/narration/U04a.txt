Numbers first for precision, plots second for shape, then check that the two agree.

L:5 | Missing value check | The very first non-graphical check on any dataset is counting empty cells, before any statistic or plot. One line does it for every column at once: df dot isna dot sum.
L:6 | Missing value results | Product base margin is missing for sixty three orders, just zero point seven five percent, a small and easily flagged gap. Every one of the six numerical features we use today has zero missing values, and the categorical features are complete too.
L:8 | Central tendency | Technique one is central tendency. The mean is the arithmetic average and is sensitive to extremes. The median is the middle value and resists extremes. The mode is the most frequent value. Each is one line for all numerical columns: mean, median, mode, no loop needed.
L:9 | Central tendency results | Profit shows the most dramatic gap of any feature: the mean is one hundred eighty one dollars while the median is minus one fifty. L:10 | Dispersion | Technique two is dispersion. Standard deviation is the typical spread from the mean. Range is maximum minus minimum and is sensitive to one extreme value. The I Q R is Q three minus Q one, the spread of the middle fifty percent, and resists extremes. Again one line computes each statistic for every feature.
L:11 | Dispersion results | Sales has a standard deviation of thirty five eighty five, more than twice its I Q R of fifteen sixty six. A handful of very large orders are stretching the spread. Profit's standard deviation is over four times its I Q R, the same pattern for gains and losses. Order quantity's standard deviation and I Q R are in sensible proportion: well-behaved dispersion.
L:12 | Shape | Technique three is shape. Skewness of zero means symmetric, positive means a long right tail, and the further from zero the more lopsided. Kurtosis measures how heavy the tails are: very high kurtosis means extreme values are far more common than a normal distribution predicts. Skew and kurtosis are one line each for every feature.
L:13 | Shape results | Unit price is extraordinarily skewed, fourteen point one three, with kurtosis of two seventy one, the most extreme feature. Sales, skew five point four, and profit, skew three point six five, are also dramatically skewed. Order quantity and discount sit almost exactly at zero, genuinely symmetric.
L:14 | Skewness ranked | Ranked by skewness, unit price towers over every other feature, so extreme that it barely fits on the same chart. Order quantity and discount sit at zero. L:16 | The histogram | Now graphical E D A for numerical variables. Plot type one is the histogram. It splits the range into bins and counts how many rows fall into each, the most direct picture of a distribution's shape. Tall bars mean common values. It is the best way to take a first, honest look at any numerical column.
L:17 | Histograms of all six | Order quantity and discount show flat, evenly spread bars, visually confirming their near-zero skewness. Sales, unit price and shipping cost show one towering bar near zero and an almost invisible tail, the picture behind their extreme skewness numbers. And profit shows a sharp spike near zero with real mass on both the negative and positive side, confirming the loss-making orders.
L:18 | The density plot | Plot type two is the density plot, built with kernel density estimation. It is a smoothed histogram, a continuous curve instead of bars. It removes the arbitrary choice of bin width and gives a cleaner picture, but smoothing can hide small real spikes that a histogram shows, so the two complement each other.
L:19 | Density plots of all six | Order quantity's curve confirms a genuinely flat, near-uniform shape. Profit shows a sharp, narrow peak straddling zero, distinct from the one-sided skew of sales or unit price. Sales and unit price again collapse into a flat line squashed against zero. Density plots handle moderate skew well, but not this level of it.
L:20 | The boxplot | Plot type three is the boxplot. It packs five numbers into one picture: minimum, Q one, median, Q three and maximum, plus individually plotted outlier points. The box spans the middle fifty percent, whiskers show the normal range, and dots beyond them are flagged outliers. It is best for comparing spread and spotting outliers across several features side by side.
L:21 | Boxplots of all six | Order quantity and discount have honest, well-formed boxes with essentially no outliers. Profit's box straddles zero with outlier dots stretching both ways, large losses on one side and large gains on the other. Sales, unit price and shipping cost have boxes crushed toward zero, exactly as their dispersion numbers warned.
L:22 | The violin plot | Plot type four is the violin plot. It mirrors a density curve on both sides of a central boxplot, so you see the full distribution shape and the five-number summary together. A boxplot alone can hide whether data is single-peaked, multi-peaked or lopsided; the violin reveals it. Use it when the shape itself is part of the story.
L:23 | Violin plots of all six | Order quantity has the most rectangular, even violin body, the clearest visual confirmation of a uniform shape. Profit's violin is narrow and symmetric around zero, unlike the one-sided needles of sales or unit price. Sales, unit price and shipping cost appear as needle-thin spikes. The violin plot cannot rescue a shape this extreme any more than the density plot could.
L:24 | Distribution comparisons | Plot type five overlays the same feature's density curve for several categories. Curves that overlap heavily mean the category barely affects the feature; curves that separate mean the category is a real driver. It is an early visual check of whether a categorical feature is worth investigating as a predictor.
L:25 | Profit by product category | All three categories centre close to zero, with gains and losses in each. Technology shows a visibly wider spread, bigger wins and bigger losses. Average profit confirms it: technology averages four hundred twenty nine dollars per order, more than six times furniture's sixty eight dollars.
L:26 | Shipping cost by ship mode | Delivery truck shows the widest spread and highest typical shipping cost. Regular air and express air cluster at lower costs, with express air slightly higher. This is a sensible pattern: heavier or bulkier shipments that need truck delivery naturally cost more.
L:28 | Comparison: unit price | Now compare the two approaches. For unit price, the non-graphical view said skewness fourteen, kurtosis two seventy one, mean more than four times the median, ten percent of rows flagged as outliers. The graphical view showed one towering bar, a boxplot crushed flat with a wall of outlier dots past sixty seven hundred dollars, and a needle violin. Combined: most items are inexpensive, but a small set of high-end products pulls every average sharply upward.
L:29 | Comparison: order quantity | For order quantity, skewness is about zero, mean and median sit almost on top of each other, kurtosis is negative one point two one, and zero percent are outliers. The histogram is rectangular across one to fifty, the boxplot has no outlier dots, and the violin is the most uniform. The number and the picture agree closely, a rare, reassuring case.
L:30 | The overall lesson | The overall lesson. When they agree, as with order quantity, you can trust either technique. When they diverge in detail, as with unit price, the skewness number warned of extreme skew, but only the boxplot showed that the entire spread lives in the outlier dots, not the box. Non-graphical statistics are fast and precise but abstract; plots are intuitive but can hide detail. Always use both together.
L:32 | Frequency tables | Now categorical variables. Plot type six is the frequency table: a count and percentage for each category, the categorical equivalent of describe. The biggest number is the most common category, and a category with a tiny count is a warning for later grouped analysis. It is the very first check on any categorical column.

L:34 | The bar plot | Plot type seven is the bar plot. It turns a frequency table into a picture, one bar per category, so the eye compares heights far faster than numbers in a table. It is best for presenting frequencies, or for comparing a summary statistic, like average profit, across categories.
L:35 | Bar plots of all three | Product category shows office supplies towering over the other two. Ship mode shows a steep drop after regular air, since most orders default to standard shipping. Customer segment shows a real but modest lead for corporate over three fairly even segments.
L:36 | The pie chart | Plot type eight is the pie chart, a part-to-whole view. A caution: pie charts get hard to read past four or five slices, and are poor at comparing similar-sized slices. L:37 | Product category pie | Office supplies alone accounts for well over half of every order. Technology and furniture together make up less than half, despite being two categories. Seeing this part-to-whole makes the dominance of office supplies easier to feel than the bar plot alone.
L:39 | Detecting anomalies | Finally, anomalies. The rule: compute Q one, Q three and the I Q R, and flag anything below Q one minus one point five I Q R, or above Q three plus one point five I Q R. This non-graphical rule has a graphical twin: the same fences are what a boxplot draws as whiskers. And not every outlier is an error: a twenty seven thousand dollar profit and a fourteen thousand dollar loss are both genuine business events.
L:40 | Anomalies counted | Profit has one thousand seven hundred four outliers, twenty percent of rows, the highest rate, capturing both large gains and large losses. Profit's outliers are exactly the extreme wins and losses that help explain why over half of orders lose money.

The techniques agree on order quantity and reinforce each other on the skewed features: the numbers flag that something is unusual, and the plots show exactly what and why.
