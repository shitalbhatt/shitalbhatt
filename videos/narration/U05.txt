@video L09-10_Bivariate_Analysis
L:1 | Introduction | Welcome to lectures nine and ten: bivariate analysis, the relationships between two variables. We cover correlation, covariance, scatterplots and categorical relationships.
L:2 | Three pair types | Every pair of features is one of three types. Numerical with numerical, like sales and profit, uses correlation, covariance and scatterplots. Categorical with categorical, like segment and ship mode, uses cross-tabs, heatmaps and stacked bars. And categorical with numerical, like category and profit, uses grouped statistics and boxplots.
L:3 | Why two variables | Why look at two variables together? Univariate statistics miss the story: one feature's mean never tells you how it behaves alongside another. It reveals hidden drivers: discount alone looks harmless, but discount against profit shows heavy discounting erodes margin. It uncovers structure in categories. And it feeds the scientific process, since every does X affect Y question starts as a bivariate check.
L:4 | Covariance | Covariance asks whether two variables move together. For every data point, multiply how far X is from its mean by how far Y is from its mean, then average. Positive covariance means they rise together, negative means one rises as the other falls, and near zero means no consistent linear pattern. The catch is that covariance is in the product of the two units, like dollars times units, so its size cannot be compared across pairs.
L:5 | Correlation | Correlation is the scale-free version. Divide covariance by the product of the standard deviations and you get r, always between minus one and plus one regardless of units. That makes coefficients comparable across every pair in a matrix. Roughly, beyond plus or minus zero point seven is strong, between zero point three and zero point seven is moderate to weak, and near zero means no linear relation.
L:6 | Correlation is not causation | A strong correlation never proves that one variable causes the other. Both may be driven by a hidden third factor, or the pattern may be a coincidence in this sample. For example, discount and profit are correlated, but so are sales and profit. Before acting on a single correlation, check what else might explain it.
L:7 | Spearman correlation | Pearson measures straight-line relationships only and is sensitive to outliers. Spearman ranks the values first, then correlates the ranks, so it captures any monotonic relationship and is more robust. In the Superstore data, discount versus profit is minus zero point two two by Pearson but minus zero point five four by Spearman: a handful of heavily discounted, deeply negative-profit orders were flattening the straight-line number.
L:8 | Scatterplots | A scatterplot places every order as one dot, one feature on X and another on Y. The tilt of the cloud shows direction, and how tightly dots hug a line shows strength. Unlike a single r value, a scatterplot also reveals outliers, clusters and non-linear shapes that a correlation number can hide.
L:9 | Scatterplot gallery | Here is a gallery of patterns: positive linear, negative linear, no relationship, curvilinear, a fanning spread where variability grows, known as heteroscedasticity, and an outlier-driven pattern. The same r can come from very different shapes, so always look.
L:10 | Matrix and heatmap | A correlation matrix stacks the correlation between every pair of numeric features into one table. A heatmap colours that table, darker green for strong positive and darker red for strong negative, so patterns across many features jump out. The diagonal is always one, and the matrix is symmetric.
L:11 | Cross-tabulation | A cross-tab, or contingency table, counts how many records fall into every combination of two categorical features. It is the categorical equivalent of a correlation matrix. Raw counts show volume, while row percentages let you compare the ship mode mix within each segment on the same zero to one hundred scale. Here every segment leans standard class at almost the same rate.
L:12 | Reading a cross-tab | If every row has roughly the same percentage split, the two features do not seem related: knowing one tells you nothing extra about the other. If the splits differ noticeably, the features are associated. Segment versus ship mode lands near sixty percent standard class in every row, so it looks independent. A visible pattern like this is what later becomes a chi-square test of independence.
L:13 | Comparing a number across groups | For categorical against numerical, there are three tools. Grouped summary statistics: split by category, then compute mean, median and standard deviation within each group. Boxplots by category: the full distribution per group, showing spread and not just average. And a bar chart of group means for a quick comparison of central tendency.
L:14 | Which technique for which pair | Which technique for which pair? Numerical with numerical: covariance and correlation, scatterplot or pairplot; watch for non-linear shapes and outliers. Categorical with categorical: cross-tab counts and percentages, heatmap or stacked bar; watch for rare categories with tiny counts. Categorical with numerical: grouped statistics, boxplot or bar chart; with skewed groups the median can beat the mean. Identify each column's type first, and that tells you which row to use.
L:15 | Common pitfalls | Four pitfalls. Spurious correlation: unrelated features can correlate by chance, especially in small samples, so ask if it makes real-world sense. Outlier-driven correlation: a handful of extreme points can create or hide a correlation, so pair the number with a scatterplot. Simpson's paradox: a trend in the whole dataset can reverse within each subgroup. And small subgroup counts can make a cross-tab percentage misleading.
L:16 | Key takeaways | Key takeaways. Start by identifying the type of each feature. Covariance shows direction; correlation rescales it so pairs are comparable. Pearson catches linear patterns; Spearman catches any monotonic pattern and resists outliers. A correlation matrix and heatmap summarise all pairs, and scatterplots show what one number can hide. Cross-tabs are the categorical correlation matrix. And correlation is not causation: every finding is the seed of a hypothesis to test later. Next is the lab.

P:1 | Introduction | Welcome to practicals nine and ten: bivariate analysis in Python. We build a correlation matrix, heatmaps and cross-tabs, and generate hypotheses, using the Superstore orders dataset.
P:2 | Setup | First load the dataset and import pandas, numpy, seaborn and matplotlib. We use the latin one encoding to handle special characters, and drop the few rows with a blank segment.
P:3 | Identify feature types | Next identify numeric and categorical features. Select dtypes with include number picks every numeric column in one command; then we drop postal code, because it is a number but an I D, not a measurement. The numeric columns are sales, quantity, discount and profit. For categoricals we choose columns with a manageable number of categories: ship mode, segment, region, category and sub-category.
P:4 | Covariance matrix | One command, cov, computes covariance between every pair of the four numeric features at once. Notice the units: the covariance of sales and profit is about seventy thousand, and the sign is meaningful, but the huge number is not comparable to the covariance of discount and profit, about minus ten point six. That is why we compute correlation next.
P:5 | Pearson and Spearman | Correlation by default is Pearson; pass method equals spearman for ranks. Sales and profit are moderate positive, about zero point five. Sales and quantity are weak positive. And discount with profit jumps from minus zero point two two to minus zero point five four once we rank the data: a few heavily discounted, deeply negative-profit orders were flattening the Pearson number.
P:6 | Heatmap | A heatmap of the correlation matrix is one seaborn call. Annot true prints each value, and fixing the colour scale from minus one to plus one makes intensity always comparable.
P:7 | Scatterplot and pairplot | A scatterplot shows one pair and the shape a correlation number can hide. A pairplot draws a scatterplot for every pair of numeric features, plus a histogram per feature on the diagonal, covering all pairs at once with no loop.
P:8 | Cross-tabulation | Pandas crosstab counts orders in every segment by ship mode combination. Normalize equals index turns each row into percentages, comparing ship-mode mix within each segment fairly. All three segments cluster near fifty nine to sixty percent standard class, so shipping preference looks similar across segments.
P:9 | Cross-tab heatmap and stacked bar | The same shading idea applies to a cross-tab: pass it to a heatmap. And calling plot with kind bar and stacked true directly on a cross-tab turns counts into a stacked bar chart in one line.
P:10 | Grouped statistics | For categorical against numerical, group by category and take the mean of all four numeric columns in one line, or aggregate mean and standard deviation. Technology has the highest average profit despite the lowest average discount, while furniture has the lowest average profit.
P:11 | Boxplots and bar chart | A boxplot of profit by category shows the full distribution per group. A bar plot with the mean estimator compares average sales by segment at a glance.
P:12 | Hypothesis generation | Now turn findings into hypotheses. H one: discount hurts profit, with Spearman r of minus zero point five four, strongest in the Central region. H two: category drives profitability more than sales volume. H three: ship-mode preference is independent of customer segment. H four: regional discounting policy varies, a policy effect and not a product-mix effect. These are hypotheses to test, not conclusions.
P:13 | Worked example: Titanic | The same six commands work on a different dataset, Titanic. Correlation of age, fare and family counts; a cross-tab of sex by survived with row percentages; grouped means by passenger class; and a boxplot of fare by class. Female passengers survived at a far higher rate than male passengers, and first-class passengers paid far higher fares. Same pattern, new column names.
P:14 | Cheat sheet | Your code cheat sheet. Numerical with numerical: cov, corr with optional Spearman, a heatmap, scatterplot or pairplot. Categorical with categorical: crosstab with normalize, a heatmap, a stacked bar. Categorical with numerical: group by with mean or aggregate, boxplot, bar plot. Try these on your own dataset.
