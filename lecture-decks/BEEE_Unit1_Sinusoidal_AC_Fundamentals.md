| 3 | Recap: What You Already Know | • DC: v and i are constant in time.<br>• Ohm's law: V = I·R; power P = V·I.<br>• Faraday's law: e = −N·dφ/dt.<br>• Trigonometry: sin, cos, radians and degrees.<br>**Formula:** e = −N·dφ/dt<br>e induced EMF (V), N turns, φ flux (Wb) | **Layout:** 3 cards.<br>Card 1: DC battery and constant line graph.<br>Card 2: coil with a magnet moving in and out, labelled "EMF".<br>Card 3: unit circle with 0°, 90°, 180°, 270° and radian values. | Quick recap. | 4 | Motivation: What Does "230 V" Mean? | • Wall socket: 230 V, 50 Hz.<br>• That 230 V is an RMS value, not peak.<br>• The actual peak voltage is about 325 V.<br>• AC is easy to step up and down with transformers.<br>**Formula:** Vm = √2·V = 1.414 × 230 ≈ 325 V | **Layout:** photo of a wall socket with "230 V, 50 Hz" label on the left.<br>Right: sine wave with Vm = 325 V peak marked and a dashed line at 230 V, labelled "RMS: equivalent DC heating value". | Ask: if the socket says 230 volts, why does the wave reach 325 volts? Because 230 is the equivalent heating value of the wave, not its peak. This is exactly why we need RMS. Also mention that AC wins in power systems because transformers change voltage levels cheaply for long-distance transmission. |
| 5 | Generation of Sinusoidal Voltage | • Coil of N turns rotates in a uniform magnetic field.<br>• Flux linked: φ = Φm cos ωt.<br>• EMF induced: e = Em sin ωt.<br>• EMF is zero when the coil is perpendicular to the field.<br>• EMF is maximum when the coil is parallel to the field.<br>**Formulas:** Em = N·B·A·ω<br>N turns, B flux density (T), A coil area (m²), ω speed (rad/s) | **Layout:** left: 2-pole magnet (N and S) with a rectangular coil, slip rings and brushes, rotation arrow.<br>Right: sine wave of e against ωt (0° to 360°).<br>Mark 4 coil positions (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°) with small coil sketches joined by lines to points on the wave. | A coil turning in a magnetic field cuts flux, and by Faraday's law an EMF appears. The flux is Φm cos ωt, so e is minus N dφ/dt, which gives N B A ω sin ωt. At 0° the coil sides move along the field lines and EMF is zero. At 90° they cut the field at right angles and EMF is maximum. The slip rings and brushes bring the voltage out to the load. |
| 6 | Basic Terms: Cycle, Period, Frequency | • Cycle: one complete set of positive and negative values.<br>• Period T: time for one cycle (s).<br>• Frequency f: cycles per second (Hz).<br>• Angular frequency ω: radians per second.<br>• Generator: f = P·Ns/120 (P poles, Ns rpm).<br>**Formulas:** f = 1/T; ω = 2πf<br>For 50 Hz: T = 20 ms, ω = 314.16 rad/s | **Layout:** one sine wave with one full cycle shaded, T marked on the time axis.<br>Below: conversion strip "50 Hz → T = 20 ms → ω = 314 rad/s".<br>Small inset: 4-pole machine at 1500 rpm = 50 Hz. | Define each term using the same picture. One complete wave is a cycle, and the time it takes is the period. Frequency is the inverse. Because one cycle is 2π radians, ω is 2π times f. For India, 50 Hz means 20 milliseconds per cycle. A 4-pole machine turning at 1500 rpm gives 4 × 1500 / 120 = 50 Hz. |
| 7 | Equation of a Sinusoid and Phase | • General form: v = Vm sin(ωt + φ).<br>• φ positive: wave leads the reference.<br>• φ negative: wave lags the reference.<br>• φ = 0°: in phase; φ = 90°: quadrature; φ = 180°: opposition.<br>**Formula:** v = Vm sin(ωt + φ), i = Im sin(ωt − φ)<br>Vm peak (V), φ phase angle (rad or °) | **Layout:** two waves on one plot: v (blue) starting at 0, i (red) shifted right by φ.<br>Mark φ as a horizontal double arrow between the two zero crossings, labelled "i lags v by φ".<br>Small side table: in-phase, quadrature, antiphase. | The phase angle tells us how far one wave is shifted relative to another. If the wave reaches its peak earlier, we say it leads. If it reaches later, it lags. This idea will come back strongly when we study R, L and C circuits in the next lecture. Check with students: which wave in the plot is leading? |
| 8 | Average Value | • Average of a full sine cycle is zero.<br>• So average is defined over a half cycle.<br>• It equals the height of a rectangle of equal area.<br>• Used for rectified signals and DC meters.<br>**Formulas:** Vav = (1/T)∫v dt<br>Half-cycle: Vav = (1/π)∫₀^π Vm sin θ dθ = 2Vm/π = 0.637·Vm | **Layout:** one half-cycle of a sine wave shaded.<br>Overlay a rectangle of the same area with its height labelled Vav = 0.637 Vm and width π.<br>Small note: "Full cycle: positive area = negative area, so Vav = 0". | The average is the area under the curve divided by the length of the base. For a full sine cycle the positive and negative areas cancel, so the average is zero, which is not very useful. Therefore we take the average over a half cycle. The integral of sin from 0 to π is 2, so Vav equals 2 Vm over π, which is 0.637 Vm. |
| 9 | RMS (Effective) Value | • RMS: root of the mean of the squares.<br>• Equals the DC value giving the same heating.<br>• For a sine: v² has mean Vm²/2.<br>• Meters and ratings use the RMS value.<br>**Formulas:** V = √[(1/T)∫v² dt]<br>Sine: V = Vm/√2 = 0.707·Vm<br>Identity: sin²θ = (1 − cos 2θ)/2 | **Layout:** top: sine wave v(t). Below it: v² curve (always positive, twice the frequency) with a dashed mean line at Vm²/2.<br>Right: a lamp heated by AC and an identical lamp by DC, equal brightness, with the label "V_DC = V_RMS". | We square the wave, take the mean, and then take the square root. Squaring gives sin² which can be rewritten using the double-angle identity, and the cosine part averages to zero. That leaves Vm² over 2, so the RMS value is Vm over root 2. The key idea is heat: a 230 V RMS supply heats a resistor exactly like 230 V DC. |
| 10 | Form Factor and Peak Factor | • Form factor = RMS value / average value.<br>• Peak factor = peak value / RMS value.<br>• They describe the shape of a waveform.<br>• For a sine wave: form factor 1.11, peak factor 1.414.<br>**Formulas:** Kf = V/Vav = 1.11; Kp = Vm/V = √2 = 1.414<br>Table (sine): V = 0.707 Vm; Vav = 0.637 Vm | **Layout:** one sine wave with three horizontal levels drawn: Vm (top), V = 0.707 Vm, Vav = 0.637 Vm.<br>Right: small table with the four numbers and the formulas.<br>Footnote: "Valid only for a sine wave". | These two numbers describe the shape of the waveform. For a sine, 0.707 divided by 0.637 gives 1.11, and the peak factor is simply 1.414. A rectified-reading AC meter is calibrated using 1.11, so it reads wrong for non-sine waves. We will use form factor again when we study rectifiers in Unit 3. |
| 11 | Phasor Representation | • A phasor is a rotating line representing a sinusoid.<br>• Length = RMS value; angle = phase angle.<br>• Vertical projection gives the instantaneous value.<br>• Same frequency waves keep a fixed angle between them.<br>**Formulas:** v = Vm sin(ωt + φ) ⇄ **V** = V∠φ<br>V RMS magnitude (V), φ phase (°) | **Layout:** left: phasor diagram with a vector V at angle φ and an arrow showing counter-clockwise rotation at ω.<br>Right: the corresponding sine wave, with a horizontal dotted line from the tip of the phasor to the wave (projection).<br>Show two positions of the phasor linked to two points on the wave. | Phasors turn a time-varying wave into a simple arrow. The length of the arrow is the RMS value and the angle is the phase. As the arrow rotates counter-clockwise at ω, its vertical projection traces the sine wave. Since every wave in a circuit has the same ω, we freeze the picture and compare only magnitude and angle. |
| 12 | Working with Phasors | • Rectangular form: V = a + jb.<br>• Polar form: V = ∣V∣∠θ.<br>• Add and subtract in rectangular form.<br>• Multiply and divide in polar form.<br>• Phasors add only if frequencies are equal.<br>**Formulas:** ∣V∣ = √(a² + b²); θ = tan⁻¹(b/a)<br>a = ∣V∣cos θ; b = ∣V∣sin θ | **Layout:** complex plane (real axis a, imaginary axis jb).<br>Show phasor V with its components a and b, magnitude ∣V∣ and angle θ.<br>Second small diagram: parallelogram sum of V1 and V2 giving V.<br>Corner note: "j = 90° rotation". | Convert between the two forms with Pythagoras and the tangent. Addition is easy in rectangular form; multiplication is easy in polar form. A common error is adding magnitudes directly, which is wrong unless the phasors are in phase. Watch the quadrant when you take the inverse tangent. |
| 13 | Solved Example 1: Values of a Sine Wave | **Given:** v = 325 sin(314t) volts. Find Vm, V, f, T, Vav (half cycle), and v at t = 2.5 ms.<br>1. Vm = 325 V.<br>2. V = Vm/√2 = 325/1.414 = 229.8 ≈ 230 V.<br>3. ω = 314 rad/s → f = ω/2π = 314/6.283 ≈ 50 Hz.<br>4. T = 1/f = 20 ms.<br>5. Vav = 0.637 × 325 = 207 V.<br>6. At t = 2.5 ms: ωt = 314 × 0.0025 = 0.785 rad = 45°.<br>7. v = 325 sin 45° = 325 × 0.707 ≈ 230 V. | **Layout:** left: numbered steps, each revealed on click.<br>Right: the sine wave with Vm = 325 V, V = 230 V, Vav = 207 V marked as horizontal lines, and the point at t = 2.5 ms (45°) highlighted. | This is the mains voltage from our motivation slide. Read the peak and ω straight from the equation, then use the formulas one at a time. Ask students to find f before showing the answer. Notice that at 45° the instantaneous value happens to be about 230 V, equal to the RMS value. This is a good coincidence to point out. |
| 14 | Solved Example 2: Adding Two Voltages with Phasors | **Given:** v1 = 141.4 sin ωt V, v2 = 84.9 sin(ωt + 60°) V. Find v1 + v2.<br>1. Convert to RMS: V1 = 100∠0° V, V2 = 60∠60° V.<br>2. V2 rectangular: 60 cos 60° + j60 sin 60° = 30 + j51.96 V.<br>3. V = V1 + V2 = 130 + j51.96 V.<br>4. ∣V∣ = √(130² + 51.96²) = √19600 = 140 V.<br>5. θ = tan⁻¹(51.96/130) = 21.8°.<br>6. V = 140∠21.8° V (RMS).<br>7. Peak: Vm = 140 × √2 = 198 V.<br>8. **Answer:** v = 198 sin(ωt + 21.8°) V. | **Layout:** left: steps.<br>Right: phasor diagram drawn to scale: V1 along the real axis (100), V2 at 60° (60), parallelogram completed, resultant V at 21.8° and length 140.<br>Dimensions labelled on each side. | Always convert to RMS first, because our phasors use RMS lengths. Then convert V2 to rectangular form, add the real and imaginary parts, and convert back to polar. Check with the law of cosines: 100² + 60² + 2·100·60·cos60° = 19600, so the magnitude is 140 V. Finally, convert back to peak if the question asks for the time-domain expression. |
| 15 | Common Mistakes and Exam Tips | • Mixing up peak and RMS values.<br>• Calculator left in degree mode for ωt in radians.<br>• Using 0.637 Vm for a full cycle; it is zero.<br>• Applying 1.11 and 1.414 to non-sine waves.<br>• Adding phasor magnitudes directly.<br>• Half-wave rectified sine: Vav = Vm/π, V = Vm/2, Kf = 1.57, Kp = 2. | **Layout:** two-column "Mistake / Fix" table with red cross and green tick icons.<br>Bottom strip: one highlighted tip, "Always write: given → formula → substitution → answer with unit". | These are the points where marks are lost every year. Check whether the question gives you a peak value or an RMS value before you start. Tell students that ratings on equipment and voltmeter readings are RMS. The half-wave rectified wave is a favourite exam question, so memorise those four numbers. |
