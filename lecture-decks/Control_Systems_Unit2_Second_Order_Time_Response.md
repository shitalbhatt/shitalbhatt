| 5 | Standard Second-Order Model | • Servo with unity feedback gives this form.<br>• Two parameters decide the whole response.<br>• ωn: how fast the system oscillates.<br>• ζ: how quickly oscillations die out.<br>**Formulas:**<br>C(s)/R(s) = ωn² / (s² + 2ζωn·s + ωn²)<br>G(s) = ωn² / [s(s + 2ζωn)]<br>ωn natural frequency (rad/s), ζ damping ratio (–) | **Layout:** block diagram on top, formula strip below.<br>Diagram: R(s) → summing junction (+/−) → G(s) = ωn²/[s(s+2ζωn)] → C(s); a unity feedback line from C(s) to the junction labelled "−".<br>Highlight the denominator in a coloured box. | Here is our standard form. Every second-order problem in the exam is first rewritten into this form. Compare coefficients: the s² term has coefficient 1, the s term gives 2ζωn, and the constant gives ωn². Remember the open-loop form G(s) also, because a servo gives it directly. Write this on the board and keep it visible all lecture. |
| 6 | Poles and Damping Cases | • Poles: s = −ζωn ± jωn√(1−ζ²)<br>• ζ = 0: undamped, poles on imaginary axis.<br>• 0 < ζ < 1: underdamped, complex-conjugate poles.<br>• ζ = 1: critically damped, repeated real poles.<br>• ζ > 1: overdamped, two distinct real poles.<br>**Formula:** s1,2 = −ζωn ± jωd<br>ωd = ωn√(1−ζ²) (rad/s) | **Layout:** 2×2 grid of small s-plane sketches.<br>Axes: σ horizontal, jω vertical.<br>(a) two × on the jω axis at ±jωn<br>(b) two × in the left half-plane, complex pair<br>(c) one double × on the negative real axis<br>(d) two × on the negative real axis, apart.<br>Label each with its ζ range. | The roots of the denominator decide everything. When ζ is zero the poles sit on the imaginary axis and the output never dies out. As ζ increases the poles move into the left half-plane and the oscillation decays. At ζ equal to one they meet on the real axis. Beyond that, they split apart and there is no oscillation at all. |
| 7 | Underdamped Step Response | • Most useful case for design and exam.<br>• Response oscillates and settles at 1.<br>• Envelope decays as e^(−ζωn·t).<br>• Damped frequency ωd is below ωn.<br>**Formulas:**<br>c(t) = 1 − [e^(−ζωn·t)/√(1−ζ²)]·sin(ωd·t + φ)<br>φ = cos⁻¹(ζ) (rad)<br>ωd = ωn√(1−ζ²) (rad/s) | **Layout:** one large plot, full width.<br>Axes: t (s) horizontal, c(t) vertical, final value 1 drawn as dashed line.<br>Blue response curve; dashed red envelope curves 1 ± e^(−ζωn·t)/√(1−ζ²).<br>Mark one period Td = 2π/ωd between two successive peaks. | This is the result of taking the inverse Laplace transform of C(s) equals ωn² over s times the denominator. The sine term gives the oscillation, and the exponential term gives the decay. The envelope shows how fast the swings shrink. Only remember the structure for the exam; the specification formulas come from this equation. |
| 8 | Other Damping Cases | • ζ = 0: c(t) = 1 − cos(ωn·t), sustained oscillation.<br>• ζ = 1: c(t) = 1 − (1 + ωn·t)·e^(−ωn·t)<br>• ζ > 1: slow, no overshoot, two time constants.<br>• Critical damping is the fastest without overshoot.<br>**Formula:** poles for ζ > 1: s = −ζωn ± ωn√(ζ²−1) | **Layout:** one plot, four curves from the same origin.<br>Curves labelled ζ = 0 (red, constant oscillation), ζ = 0.5 (blue, overshoot), ζ = 1 (green), ζ = 2 (orange, sluggish).<br>Common dashed line at c = 1. Legend on the right. | Compare the four curves on one graph. Undamped never settles, which is like a frictionless swing. Critical damping reaches the final value fastest without crossing it. Overdamped is safe but slow. In practice we choose slightly underdamped, because a small overshoot buys us speed. |
| 9 | Rise Time and Peak Time | • tr: time to go from 0 to 100 % of final value.<br>• tp: time to reach the first peak.<br>• Both depend on ωd; larger ωd is faster.<br>**Formulas:**<br>tr = (π − φ)/ωd, φ = cos⁻¹(ζ) in rad<br>tp = π/ωd<br>tr, tp (s); ωd (rad/s) | **Layout:** step-response plot with annotations.<br>Vertical drop lines: tr at first crossing of c = 1, tp at first maximum.<br>Horizontal dashed line at c = 1 and at c = 1 + Mp.<br>Side box: "tp = half a damped cycle". | We get rise time by setting c(t) equal to 1 and solving for the first time. That gives sine of ωd·tr + φ equal to zero. For peak time we set dc/dt to zero; the first solution is ωd·tp equal to π. So the peak is exactly half a damped cycle. Be careful: use ωd, not ωn. |
| 10 | Peak Overshoot | • Mp: how far the peak goes above the final value.<br>• Depends only on ζ, not on ωn.<br>• Smaller ζ gives larger overshoot.<br>**Formulas:**<br>Mp = e^(−πζ/√(1−ζ²)); %Mp = 100·Mp<br>ζ = ln(1/Mp) / √(π² + ln²(1/Mp))<br>Table: ζ = 0.2→52.7 %, 0.4→25.4 %, 0.5→16.3 %, 0.6→9.5 %, 0.7→4.6 %, 0.8→1.5 % | **Layout:** left: step plot with Mp marked as a vertical double arrow between c = 1 and the peak.<br>Right: small plot of %Mp (vertical) vs ζ (horizontal, 0 to 1), falling curve.<br>Table of the six values under the plot. | Substitute tp into the response and you get this neat formula. Notice ωn has disappeared, so overshoot is fixed by the damping ratio alone. In design questions we are given Mp and must find ζ, so remember the inverse formula as well. For example, ζ of 0.5 gives about 16 per cent overshoot. Keep the table for quick checking. |
| 11 | Settling Time and Delay Time | • ts: time after which response stays within a band.<br>• Band: ±2 % (or ±5 %) of final value.<br>• Decided by the real part of the poles, σ = ζωn.<br>**Formulas:**<br>ts ≈ 4/(ζωn) for 2 % band<br>ts ≈ 3/(ζωn) for 5 % band<br>td ≈ (1 + 0.7ζ)/ωn (time to reach 50 %) | **Layout:** step plot with a shaded ±2 % tolerance band around c = 1.<br>Mark ts where the curve enters the band for the last time.<br>Dashed envelope curve touches the band edge at ts.<br>Inset: "e^(−ζωn·ts) ≈ 0.02 → ζωn·ts ≈ 4". | The envelope decays as e to the minus ζωn t. We ask when it falls to 2 per cent, and that gives ζωn·ts about 4. The 5 per cent band gives 3. Always say which criterion you use in the exam. Delay time is the time to reach half of the final value and is a rough guide only. |
| 12 | Pole Location and Specifications | • ωn = distance of the pole from the origin.<br>• ζ = cos θ, with θ measured from the negative real axis.<br>• Real part σ = ζωn sets settling time.<br>• Imaginary part ωd sets peak time.<br>**Formulas:** ωn = √(σ² + ωd²); ζ = σ/ωn = cos θ | **Layout:** one large s-plane.<br>One pole at (−σ, +jωd) with its mirror pole at (−σ, −jωd).<br>Draw a line to the origin (length ωn), the angle θ from the negative real axis, and drop lines showing σ and ωd.<br>Shade the desired region: left of a vertical line (fast ts) and inside a wedge (small Mp). | This slide ties everything together. Any pole pair on the s-plane tells you the specifications at a glance. Poles further left settle faster. Poles with a steeper angle from the real axis oscillate more and overshoot more. We shall use exactly this picture again in root locus in Unit 4. |
| 13 | Effect of ζ and ωn | • Increase ζ: overshoot falls, response slower to peak.<br>• Increase ωn: all times shrink; overshoot unchanged.<br>• Typical design choice: 0.4 ≤ ζ ≤ 0.8.<br>• Trade-off: speed against smoothness. | **Layout:** two small plots side by side.<br>Left plot: same ωn, three curves with ζ = 0.3, 0.6, 1.0.<br>Right plot: same ζ, three curves with ωn = 1, 2, 4 rad/s (time axis compressed).<br>Captions: "ζ changes shape", "ωn changes speed". | Keep this picture in your head. Damping ratio changes the shape of the curve, and natural frequency only stretches it in time. If a designer wants faster response without more overshoot, raise ωn and keep ζ fixed. In practice that costs more control effort, and that is why there is always a trade-off. |
| 14 | Steady-State Link (Type 1 System) | • G(s) = ωn²/[s(s+2ζωn)] is a Type 1 system.<br>• Unit step input: ess = 0.<br>• Unit ramp input: ess = 1/Kv, finite.<br>**Formulas:**<br>Kv = lim(s→0) s·G(s) = ωn/(2ζ)<br>ess(ramp) = 2ζ/ωn<br>Kv velocity error constant (1/s), ess steady-state error | **Layout:** two small plots stacked.<br>Top: step input and output meeting at final value (ess = 0).<br>Bottom: ramp input and output parallel with a constant gap labelled ess.<br>Right side: boxed formulas. | This slide connects the transient part to the steady-state part of Unit 2. Our servo has one integrator, so it follows a step exactly. For a ramp it follows with a constant lag of 2ζ over ωn. Notice the tension: raising ζ increases ramp error, while it reduces overshoot. We will meet static error constants again in the next lecture. |
| 15 | Solved Example 1: Find the Specifications | **Given:** G(s) = 25/[s(s+6)], unity feedback, unit step. Find ωn, ζ, tr, tp, Mp, ts, ess(ramp).<br>1. Closed loop: 25/(s² + 6s + 25).<br>2. ωn² = 25 → ωn = 5 rad/s.<br>3. 2ζωn = 6 → ζ = 0.6.<br>4. ωd = 5√(1−0.36) = 4 rad/s.<br>5. φ = cos⁻¹0.6 = 0.927 rad.<br>6. tr = (π − 0.927)/4 = 0.554 s.<br>7. tp = π/4 = 0.785 s.<br>8. Mp = e^(−π·0.6/0.8) = 0.0948 → 9.5 %.<br>9. ts (2 %) = 4/3 = 1.33 s; (5 %) = 3/3 = 1.0 s.<br>10. ess(ramp) = 2ζ/ωn = 0.24 | **Layout:** two columns.<br>Left: numbered solution steps, one line per step, current step highlighted on click.<br>Right: the resulting step response with tr, tp, Mp, ts marked using the computed numbers (peak 1.095 at 0.785 s). | Walk through slowly. Step one: close the loop and compare with the standard form. Steps two and three give ωn and ζ directly. After that, every specification is a single formula. Ask the class to compute tp themselves before showing it. Finally, verify with MATLAB in the lab. |
| 16 | Solved Example 2: Design from Specifications | **Given:** unity feedback, G(s) = K/[s(s+a)]. Want Mp = 10 % and tp = 2 s. Find K, a, ts.<br>1. ln(1/Mp) = ln 10 = 2.303.<br>2. ζ = 2.303/√(π² + 2.303²) = 2.303/3.895 = 0.591.<br>3. ωd = π/tp = π/2 = 1.571 rad/s.<br>4. ωn = ωd/√(1−ζ²) = 1.571/0.807 = 1.947 rad/s.<br>5. K = ωn² = 3.79.<br>6. a = 2ζωn = 2.30.<br>7. ts (2 %) = 4/(ζωn) = 4/1.151 = 3.47 s. | **Layout:** flow chart on the left: Mp → ζ → (with tp) ωd → ωn → K, a.<br>Right: s-plane showing the two required pole positions at −1.15 ± j1.57 with the ωn circle and the θ line.<br>Final answer in a green box. | This is the reverse problem and examiners love it. Overshoot gives the damping ratio. Peak time and ζ together give ωn. Then match the closed-loop denominator s² + a·s + K with the standard form. Notice the pole sits at minus 1.15 plus or minus j1.57, which we can plot. Always check by computing ts. |
| 17 | Common Mistakes and Exam Tips | • Using ωn instead of ωd in tp and tr.<br>• Mixing degrees and radians for φ.<br>• Mp is a fraction; %Mp is ×100.<br>• ts formula needs the stated band, 2 % or 5 %.<br>• Overshoot formula is valid only for 0 < ζ < 1.<br>• Always rewrite into standard form first.<br>• Show formula, substitution, answer with unit. | **Layout:** two-column "Do / Don't" slide.<br>Left column (red cross icons): the mistakes.<br>Right column (green tick icons): the tips.<br>Footer: "Check: ζ between 0 and 1? units in s?" | These are the most common marks lost in the exam. Setting the calculator to radians for φ is a classic one. Also remember that the closed-loop standard form needs the constant term to match ωn squared, so check the gain. Writing units and the formula earns method marks even if the arithmetic slips. |
