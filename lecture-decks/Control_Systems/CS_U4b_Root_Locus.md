| 3 | Recap: What You Already Know | • Closed-loop poles solve 1 + GH = 0.<br>• Pole position sets the transient response.<br>• Gain K changes the pole positions.<br>• Routh gives the stable range of K.<br>**Formula/Key:** Closed-loop poles: roots of 1 + K·G(s)H(s) = 0,  with  K ≥ 0 | Root locus of K/[s(s+2)] with arrows showing poles moving as K increases. | The poles of the closed loop are the roots of one plus K G H. Routh's test told us for which K all roots are in the left half-plane, but not where they are. The root locus shows the path of every closed-loop pole as K goes from zero to infinity, so we see ζ, ωn and stability at once. |
| 4 | Motivation: Tuning the Gain | • Gain sets speed, overshoot and stability.<br>• Trial and error with step tests is slow.<br>• The locus shows all designs in one picture.<br>• Choose K to put poles where specs need them.<br>**Formula/Key:** Root locus = plot of closed-loop poles as K varies from 0 to ∞ | The same locus picture with the poles moving; emphasise the design use. | A designer may want a damping ratio of 0.5 and a given settling time. Rather than solving the characteristic equation for many values of K, we draw the root locus once. Then we pick the point on the locus that meets the specification and read the gain from it. This is the standard method used with MATLAB's rlocus. |
| 5 | Root Locus Concept | • Poles of G(s)H(s) are the starting points (K = 0).<br>• Zeros of G(s)H(s) are the end points (K → ∞).<br>• Each pole traces a branch as K increases.<br>• A branch leaving to ∞ follows an asymptote.<br>**Formula/Key:** 1 + K·G(s)H(s) = 0  ⇒  K·G(s)H(s) = −1<br>G(s)H(s) = N(s)/D(s), K gain, K ≥ 0 | Locus for K/[s(s+2)]: poles at 0 and −2 move together, meet at −1 and leave vertically. | For K equal to zero the closed-loop poles are the open-loop poles. As K grows, they move along branches. For the example, the poles at zero and minus two move toward each other, meet at minus one when K is one, then split into a complex pair with constant real part minus one. All design information is on this picture. |
| 6 | Magnitude and Angle Conditions | • A point s is on the locus if it satisfies both.<br>• Angle condition: ∠GH = ±180°(2q + 1).<br>• Magnitude condition gives the gain K at s.<br>• Measure with vectors from poles and zeros.<br>**Formula/Key:** ∠G(s)H(s) = ±180°(2q + 1);   K = 1/∣G(s)H(s)∣ = (product of pole distances)/(product of zero distances) | s-plane with a test point and vectors to three poles; the angle and length conditions are written beside it. | From K G H equal to minus one we get two conditions. The angle of G H must be an odd multiple of 180 degrees, which tests whether the point is on the locus. The magnitude of K G H must equal one, which gives the value of K at that point. The magnitude is the ratio of products of distances measured from the point to the poles and zeros. |
| 7 | Rules 1–2: Branches, Start, End, Symmetry | • Number of branches = number of poles (if P ≥ Z).<br>• Branches start at poles, end at zeros or ∞.<br>• P − Z branches go to infinity.<br>• The locus is symmetrical about the real axis.<br>**Formula/Key:** P poles, Z zeros → P − Z asymptotic branches | Four cards: branches, start, end, symmetry. | Count the poles and zeros of the open-loop transfer function. Each branch starts at a pole. P minus Z of the branches go to infinity, the others terminate at the zeros. Since complex roots occur in conjugate pairs, the locus is symmetric about the real axis, so we only need to draw one half. |
| 8 | Rule 3: Real-Axis Segments | • Take a test point on the real axis.<br>• Count poles and zeros to its right.<br>• Odd count: the point is on the locus.<br>• Even count: not on the locus.<br>**Formula/Key:** Example poles 0, −2, −4: locus on [−2, 0] and (−∞, −4] | Real axis with poles at 0, −2, −4; locus segments highlighted in green with counts above. | Pick any point on the real axis and count the poles and zeros to its right. Complex ones cancel in pairs, so only the real ones matter. If the total is odd, the angle condition is met and the point is on the locus. For our three poles, the intervals between 0 and minus 2, and to the left of minus 4 qualify. |
| 9 | Rule 4: Asymptotes | • P − Z branches approach straight lines.<br>• Angles are equally spaced.<br>• All asymptotes meet at the centroid σa on the real axis.<br>• Example P = 3, Z = 0: 60°, 180°, 300°.<br>**Formula/Key:** θq = ±180°(2q + 1)/(P − Z);   σa = (Σ poles − Σ zeros)/(P − Z)<br>Example: σa = (0 − 2 − 4)/3 = −2 | Locus of K/[s(s+2)(s+4)] with three dashed asymptotes through σa = −2. | For large K the branches that do not end at finite zeros become straight lines. Their angles are odd multiples of 180 degrees divided by P minus Z. They all radiate from the centroid, which is the sum of the poles minus the sum of the zeros divided by P minus Z. In the example the centroid is minus two and the angles are 60, 180 and 300 degrees. |
| 10 | Rule 5: Breakaway and Break-in Points | • Branches leave or join the real axis.<br>• Write K = −1/(G H) as a function of s.<br>• Solve dK/ds = 0.<br>• Accept roots lying on the locus (rule 3).<br>• Compute K at the point.<br>**Formula/Key:** dK/ds = 0 with K = −D(s)/N(s)<br>Example: K = −(s³ + 6s² + 8s) → 3s² + 12s + 8 = 0 → s = −0.845 (valid), −3.155 (not on locus) | Locus with the breakaway point at −0.845 marked on the real axis. | Between two adjacent poles on the real axis the locus must break away into the complex plane. The break point is where K has a maximum along the real axis, so we set dK by ds to zero. Solve the quadratic, and keep only roots that lie on valid real-axis segments. Here minus 0.845 is on the locus, while minus 3.155 is not. |
| 11 | Rules 6–7: Departure Angle and jω Crossing | • Departure angle from a complex pole: 180° − Σ(other angles).<br>• Arrival angle at a complex zero: similar.<br>• jω crossing: put s = jω or use Routh.<br>• Crossing gives K for marginal stability.<br>**Formula/Key:** Departure angle = 180° − Σ ∠(poles) + Σ ∠(zeros)<br>Crossing: Routh row of zeros → K = 48, ω = 2.83 rad/s | Locus with the jω-axis crossing points ±j2.83 marked and the unstable region labelled. | If the poles are complex, the direction in which the branch leaves can be found from the angle condition at that pole. The imaginary-axis crossing is most easily found using Routh's test: at the critical gain a row of zeros appears and the auxiliary polynomial gives the frequency. For our example K equals 48 and the crossing is at j 2.83, as we found in the previous lecture. |

| 13 | Solved Example 2: Gain for ζ = 0.5 | • For the same system find K for ζ = 0.5 and the closed-loop poles.<br>• 1. ζ = 0.5 → poles on the line at 60° from the negative real axis.<br>• 2. The dominant pair is slow, so the real pole at minus 4.67 is far away and can be neglected. |
