| 3 | Recap: What You Already Know | • Phase margin relates to damping (PM ≈ 100ζ).<br>• Gain K sets Kv and steady-state error.<br>• Bandwidth sets speed of response.<br>• Bode plot: pole −20 dB/dec, zero +20 dB/dec.<br>**Formula/Key:** PM = 180° + ∠L(jωgc);   Kv = lim s·L(s)<br>L(s) open-loop transfer function | Bode plot of a lead network as a preview of what compensators do to magnitude and phase. | Recall that phase margin tells us about damping and the velocity error constant tells us about tracking accuracy. A pole adds phase lag and slopes the magnitude down, while a zero adds phase lead. A compensator is simply a deliberately chosen pole and zero pair that reshapes the open-loop frequency response. |
| 4 | Motivation: Gain Alone Is Not Enough | • Raise K: error falls but PM falls too.<br>• Specifications often conflict.<br>• Compensator adds phase or gain where needed.<br>• Simple, cheap, passive RC networks.<br>**Formula/Key:** Need: ess small and PM large and bandwidth high, at the same time | Block diagram with a compensator Gc(s) in series with the plant, highlighted in orange. | If we only change the gain, we trade accuracy against stability. A servo that meets the error requirement may oscillate. Adding a compensator changes the shape of the open-loop response so that both requirements can be met. Lab experiments nine and ten implement exactly these RC networks. |
| 5 | Series Compensation | • Compensator Gc(s) placed in the forward path.<br>• Open-loop becomes Gc(s)·G(s).<br>• Alternatives: feedback or feed-forward compensation.<br>• Lead, lag and lag-lead are the standard types.<br>**Formula/Key:** L(s) = Gc(s)·G(s);   Gc(s) = Kc·(s + z)/(s + p) | Series compensation block diagram with Gc(s) and G(s) labelled. | In series compensation the compensator is placed ahead of the plant, so the loop gain becomes the product of the two transfer functions. The compensator contains one pole and one zero whose positions determine its type. It is the most common configuration in the exam and in practice, since it is easy to realise with RC networks and operational amplifiers. |
| 6 | Lead Compensator | • Zero closer to the origin than the pole.<br>• Adds positive phase near ωm.<br>• Raises gain at high frequency.<br>• Widens bandwidth; faster, better damping.<br>• Noise at high frequency increases.<br>**Formula/Key:** Gc(s) = (1 + αTs)/(1 + Ts), α > 1;   φm = sin⁻¹[(α − 1)/(α + 1)] at ωm = 1/(T√α)<br>α lead ratio, T time constant (s) | Bode magnitude and phase of a lead network (α = 10) with the maximum phase lead marked. | The lead network has its zero at one over alpha T and its pole at one over T, so the zero is nearer the origin. The phase rises to a maximum of arcsine of alpha minus one over alpha plus one at the geometric mean of the corner frequencies. The magnitude rises by twenty log alpha at high frequency. We use it to improve phase margin and speed. |
| 7 | Lag Compensator | • Pole closer to the origin than the zero.<br>• Reduces gain at high frequency by 20 log β.<br>• Adds phase lag; place it well below ωgc.<br>• Allows a larger low-frequency gain.<br>• Reduces bandwidth; slower response.<br>**Formula/Key:** Gc(s) = (1 + Ts)/(1 + βTs), β > 1;   max lag = sin⁻¹[(β − 1)/(β + 1)] at ω = 1/(T√β)<br>β lag ratio, T (s) | Bode plot of a lag network (β = 10, T = 1 s) with the gain reduction and maximum phase lag marked. | The lag network has its pole nearer to the origin than its zero. It attenuates the high-frequency gain by twenty log beta decibels, so we can raise K to improve the steady-state error without moving the crossover frequency. The price is some phase lag, so we place the corner frequencies well below the gain crossover. Bandwidth falls, so the response becomes slower. |
| 8 | Pole–Zero Pattern: Lag vs Lead | • Lead: zero at −1/(αT), pole at −1/T.<br>• Lag: zero at −1/T, pole at −1/(βT).<br>• Lag pole–zero pair sits close to the origin.<br>• Lead pair sits near the dominant poles.<br>**Formula/Key:** Lead: ∣z∣ < ∣p∣;   Lag: ∣z∣ > ∣p∣ | Two s-plane diagrams, each with one zero (circle) and one pole (cross) on the negative real axis. | On the s-plane the only difference between lead and lag is the order of the pole and zero. For the lead network the zero is closer to the origin. For the lag network the pole is closer to the origin and the pair is placed near the origin so as not to disturb the transient response. This view is used in root-locus design. |
| 9 | Lead Design Procedure (Bode) | • Fix K for the steady-state specification.<br>• Compute the phase lead needed.<br>• Add 5°–12° to cover the gain shift.<br>• Compute α and place ωm.<br>• Verify margins and iterate.<br>**Formula/Key:** α = (1 + sin φm)/(1 − sin φm);   T = 1/(ωm√α);   gain at ωm = +10 log α dB | Six-step flow chart for designing a lead compensator. | Follow the steps in order. The needed lead is the difference between the required and the present phase margin plus a safety margin. Alpha follows from the maximum phase formula. Place the maximum lead at the new gain crossover, which is where the plant gain is minus 10 log alpha decibels. Always recheck because adding the lead shifts the crossover frequency. |
| 10 | Lag Design Procedure (Bode) | • Fix K for the steady-state specification.<br>• Find where the plant phase gives the required PM.<br>• Make that frequency the new crossover.<br>• β from the plant gain there; zero a decade below.<br>• Check the final margins.<br>**Formula/Key:** β = ∣G(jω′gc)∣;   1/T = ω′gc/10;   pole at 1/(βT) | Six-step flow chart for designing a lag compensator. | For lag design we look for the frequency where the plant already has the required phase margin plus a few degrees. The lag network must bring the magnitude to zero dB at that frequency, which fixes beta. The zero is placed about a decade below the new crossover so that the lag network adds only a few degrees of phase lag there. The pole follows from beta. |
| 11 | Lag–Lead Compensator | • Combines a lag section and a lead section.<br>• Lag improves steady-state accuracy.<br>• Lead improves speed and damping.<br>• Used when both are needed.<br>• Lag corners lie below, lead corners above.<br>**Formula/Key:** Gc(s) = [(1 + T1 s)/(1 + βT1 s)]·[(1 + T2 s)/(1 + T2 s/β)] | Bode plot of a lag–lead network showing a dip in gain at low frequency and a rise at high frequency. | When a system needs both better accuracy and better transient response, we use a lag-lead network. The lag section is placed at low frequencies and raises the low-frequency gain relative to the high-frequency gain. The lead section gives phase advance near the gain crossover. The design is done in two steps, one for each section. |
| 12 | RC Realisations and Comparison | • Lag network: R1 in series, R2–C to ground.<br>• Lead network: R1∥C in series, R2 to ground.<br>• Lead network loses gain 1/α; use an amplifier.<br>• Lab: build and test both networks.<br>**Formula/Key:** Lag: β = (R1 + R2)/R2,  T = R2C;   Lead: α = (R1 + R2)/R2,  αT = R1C | Comparison table of lead and lag (form, phase, bandwidth, speed, steady-state, noise). | The table summarises when to use each network. Use lead when the response is too slow or too oscillatory, and use lag when the accuracy is not enough but the speed is fine. The passive RC lag network is built from two resistors and one capacitor, and so is the lead network. The lead network attenuates the DC gain by one over alpha, which must be compensated by an amplifier. |
| 13 | Solved Example 1: Lead Network | • A loop has PM = 20°. Required PM = 45°; use 5° margin. Find α and T for ωm = 4 rad/s.<br>• 1. φm = 45° − 20° + 5° = 30°.<br>• 2. α = (1 + sin 30°)/(1 − sin 30°) = 1.5/0.5 = 3.<br>• 3. T = 1/(ωm√α) = 1/(4 × 1.732) = 0.144 s.<br>• 4. Zero at 1/(αT) = 2.31, pole at 1/T = 6.93 rad/s.<br>• 5. Gain at ωm = 10 log 3 = 4.77 dB.<br>**Formula/Key:** Answer: Gc(s) = (1 + 0.433s)/(1 + 0.144s);  zero 2.31, pole 6.93 rad/s | Left: steps; right: Bode plot of the designed lead with φm = 30° and +4.77 dB at ωm = 4 rad/s. | Compute the phase lead required including a safety margin of five degrees. Alpha follows from the maximum-phase formula and equals three. The geometric mean of the corner frequencies must be four rad/s, which gives the time constant. Check that the zero and pole frequencies multiply to sixteen, the square of ωm. Verify the design by recalculating the margins. |
