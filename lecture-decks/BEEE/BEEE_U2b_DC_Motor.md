| 4 | Motivation: Where DC Motors Are Used | • Electric vehicles and trains.<br>• Cranes, lifts, rolling mills.<br>• Fans, toys, robotics, wipers.<br>• Easy and wide speed control.<br>**Formula/Key:** DC motor: converts electrical power into mechanical power | Cross-section of a DC machine (yoke, poles, armature, commutator) as a preview. | DC motors are used wherever precise and wide-range speed control is needed, such as in electric traction, cranes and rolling mills. | 5 | Construction of a DC Motor | • Yoke: outer frame; carries flux.<br>• Field poles with field winding.<br>• Armature: laminated core with slots.<br>• Commutator: copper segments on the shaft.<br>• Brushes: carbon, supply current to armature.<br>**Formula/Key:** Main parts: stator (yoke + poles), rotor (armature + commutator), brushes | Cross-section of a two-pole DC machine with N and S poles, armature slots and a separate commutator sketch. | The stator consists of the yoke and the field poles carrying the field winding. The rotor is the armature, a laminated cylinder with slots that hold the armature conductors. The commutator is a ring of copper segments connected to the armature coils and the carbon brushes press against it. The commutator reverses the current in each coil as it passes the neutral axis, so that the torque stays in one direction. |
| 6 | Principle of Operation | • Armature conductors carry current in the field.<br>• Each conductor feels a force F = B·I·l.<br>• Forces on the two sides form a couple.<br>• The couple rotates the armature.<br>• Commutator keeps the torque unidirectional.<br>**Formula/Key:** F = B·I·l;   Torque T = F × r (N·m) | Conductor in a magnetic field between N and S poles with the force arrow. | When the supply is connected, current flows in the armature conductors which lie in the field of the main poles. Each conductor experiences a force, and the forces on the two sides of a coil produce a turning moment. As the coil turns, the commutator reverses the current so the force on each side stays in the same direction. The result is continuous rotation. |
| 7 | DC Shunt Motor Circuit | • Field winding connected across the supply.<br>• Field current If is nearly constant.<br>• Flux Φ is therefore constant.<br>• Armature draws Ia from the supply.<br>• Line current I = Ia + If.<br>**Formula/Key:** I = Ia + If;   If = V/Rsh | Circuit with supply V, shunt field in parallel with the armature (circle M). | In a shunt motor the field winding has many turns of thin wire and is connected in parallel with the armature across the supply voltage. The field current is small and stays practically constant, so the flux is constant. The armature current changes with the load. The line current is the sum of the two. |
| 8 | Back EMF and Its Significance | • Rotating conductors cut the field: EMF induced.<br>• This EMF opposes the supply: back EMF Eb.<br>• Ia = (V − Eb)/Ra.<br>• At start Eb = 0: very large current.<br>• Eb adjusts the current to the load: self-regulation.<br>**Formula/Key:** Eb = V − Ia·Ra = (P·Φ·Z·N)/(60·A);   Pm = Eb·Ia<br>N speed rpm, Z conductors, P poles, A parallel paths | Equivalent circuit: supply V, armature resistance Ra, back EMF Eb and current Ia, with V = Eb + IaRa. | The armature is a conductor moving in a magnetic field, so an EMF is induced in it which opposes the supply voltage, hence the name back EMF. The net voltage across the armature resistance is V minus Eb, so it controls the current. At standstill the back EMF is zero and the current can be very large, so a starter resistance is needed. When load increases, the speed falls slightly, Eb falls and more current flows: this is the self-regulating property. |
| 9 | Torque Equation | • Torque comes from flux × armature current.<br>• Shunt motor: Φ constant, T ∝ Ia.<br>• Series motor: Φ ∝ Ia, T ∝ Ia².<br>• Mechanical power = Eb·Ia = ω·T.<br>**Formula/Key:** T = (P·Z/2πA)·Φ·Ia = Kt·Φ·Ia (N·m);   Pm = Eb·Ia = 2πN·T/60<br>Φ weber, Ia ampere | Torque against armature current: straight line for shunt, parabola for series. | The torque developed depends on the flux per pole and on the armature current. For the shunt motor the flux is constant, so the torque is proportional to the armature current. The electrical power converted into mechanical power is the back EMF times the armature current, which also equals torque times angular speed. Use this to find torque from power and speed. |
| 10 | Characteristics of a Shunt Motor | • Speed falls only slightly with load.<br>• Torque rises linearly with armature current.<br>• Practically constant-speed motor.<br>• Never run without field: speed runs away.<br>**Formula/Key:** N ∝ Eb/Φ;   T ∝ Ia (Φ constant) | Three small plots: N vs Ia, T vs Ia, N vs T for a shunt motor. | Because the flux is constant and the armature resistance drop is small, the speed of a shunt motor falls only a few per cent from no load to full load. The torque is proportional to the armature current. This near-constant speed makes it suitable for lathes, fans, and pumps. If the field circuit opens, the flux nearly vanishes and the speed can rise dangerously. |
| 11 | Speed Control of a Shunt Motor | • Speed N ∝ (V − IaRa)/Φ.<br>• Armature control: vary voltage or add series R.<br>• Field control: weaken Φ with a field rheostat.<br>• Armature control gives speeds below base.<br>• Field control gives speeds above base.<br>**Formula/Key:** N = K (V − Ia·Ra)/Φ;   N2/N1 = (Eb2/Eb1)·(Φ1/Φ2)<br>K machine constant | Two cards: armature control (N rises with armature voltage) and field control (N rises as flux falls). | From the back EMF equation, speed is proportional to the armature voltage drop divided by flux. We can change the voltage applied to the armature, usually with a series resistance, which lowers the speed below the base value. Or we can weaken the field with a rheostat in series with the field winding, which raises the speed above the base value. Field control is more efficient, since the field current is small. |
| 12 | Solved Example 1: Torque and Starting | • A 220 V shunt motor, Ra = 0.5 Ω, runs at 1000 rpm taking Ia = 20 A. Find Eb, Pm, T and starting current without starter.<br>• 1. Eb = 220 − 20 × 0.5 = 210 V.<br>• 2. Pm = Eb·Ia = 4200 W.<br>• 3. ω = 2π × 1000/60 = 104.7 rad/s.<br>• 4. T = 4200/104.7 = 40.1 N·m.<br>• 5. Starting current = V/Ra = 220/0.5 = 440 A (22 × rated).<br>**Formula/Key:** Answer: Eb = 210 V, Pm = 4.2 kW, T = 40.1 N·m, Ist = 440 A without a starter | Left: steps; right: chain of four cards from Eb to Pm, ω and torque. | Subtract the armature resistance drop from the supply to get the back EMF. The electrical power converted to mechanical power is Eb times Ia, and dividing by the angular speed gives the torque. Finally note that at standstill the back EMF is zero, so the current would be 440 amperes, twenty-two times the rated value. That is why a starter is essential. |
| 13 | Solved Example 2: Speed Control | • For the same motor with constant load torque, find the speed (a) with a 1 Ω resistance added in series with the armature, (b) with the field weakened to 80 %.<br>• (a) Ia = 20 A: Eb = 220 − 20 × 1.5 = 190 V; N = 1000 × 190/210 = 905 rpm.<br>• (b) T ∝ Φ·Ia: Ia = 20/0.8 = 25 A; Eb = 220 − 25 × 0.5 = 207.5 V.<br>• N = 1000 × (207.5/210) × (1/0.8) = 1235 rpm.<br>**Formula/Key:** Answer: (a) 905 rpm,  (b) 1235 rpm | Left: steps; right: bar chart of base speed, series-resistance speed and weak-field speed. | In the first case the load torque and flux are unchanged, so the armature current stays at 20 amperes, but the extra resistor lowers the back EMF and therefore the speed. In the second case a weaker field needs a higher armature current to give the same torque. Then use the ratio of back EMFs and the inverse ratio of fluxes. Field weakening raises speed above the base value. |
