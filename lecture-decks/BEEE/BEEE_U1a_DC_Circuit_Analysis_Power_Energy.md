| 3 | Recap: What You Already Know | • Ohm's law: V = I·R.<br>• Series: same current, voltages add.<br>• Parallel: same voltage, currents add.<br>• Equivalent resistance simplifies a network.<br>**Formula/Key:** Series: Req = R1 + R2;   parallel: Req = R1R2/(R1 + R2)<br>V volt, I ampere, R ohm | Voltage divider and current divider circuits with their formulas. | Recall that in a series circuit the same current flows through every resistor and the voltages add. | 4 | Motivation: Circuits with Many Sources | • Battery banks, solar panels and chargers share loads.<br>• Series–parallel reduction fails with two sources.<br>• Systematic methods always work.<br>• Same method used by circuit simulators.<br>**Formula/Key:** Systematic methods: nodal (KCL) and mesh (KVL) | A two-source resistive network used throughout the lecture. | A solar panel and a battery connected to the same load, or two generators feeding a common bus, cannot be reduced by simple series and parallel rules. We need a systematic method that works for any network. Nodal and mesh analysis are exactly that, and they are the basis of every circuit simulator, including those used in your lab. |
| 5 | Kirchhoff's Laws | • KCL: current into a node equals current out.<br>• KVL: sum of voltage rises = sum of drops in a loop.<br>• KCL is conservation of charge.<br>• KVL is conservation of energy.<br>• Mark current directions first.<br>**Formula/Key:** KCL: ΣI(in) = ΣI(out);   KVL: ΣV = 0 around any closed loop | Left: a node with two incoming and one outgoing current; right: a loop with sources and drops. | Kirchhoff's current law says that the net current at a node is zero because charge cannot accumulate. His voltage law says that the algebraic sum of voltages around a closed loop is zero, which is conservation of energy. Fix a direction for each current at the start and keep the signs consistent: a drop occurs when you move along the current through a resistor. |
| 6 | Nodal Analysis | • Choose a reference node (ground = 0 V).<br>• Label the other node voltages.<br>• Write KCL at each non-reference node.<br>• Express branch currents with Ohm's law.<br>• Solve the equations for node voltages.<br>**Formula/Key:** (Va − V1)/R1 + Va/R3 + (Va − V2)/R2 = 0<br>Number of equations = nodes − 1 | Two-source network with node a marked; the KCL equation printed below. | In nodal analysis the unknowns are node voltages measured from a reference node. At each non-reference node we write that the sum of currents leaving through the resistors is zero. A branch current leaving a node is the node voltage minus the neighbour voltage divided by the resistance. For our network only one equation is needed, since there is just one non-reference node. |
| 7 | Mesh Analysis | • Draw a clockwise mesh current in each loop.<br>• Write KVL around each mesh.<br>• Shared resistor carries the difference of currents.<br>• Solve the equations for mesh currents.<br>• Branch currents follow from the mesh currents.<br>**Formula/Key:** (R1 + R3)i1 − R3·i2 = V1;   −R3·i1 + (R2 + R3)i2 = −V2<br>Number of equations = number of meshes | Same network with two clockwise mesh currents i1 and i2 and the two KVL equations. | In mesh analysis the unknowns are circulating currents in the independent loops. For each mesh the sum of resistance times current drops equals the source voltage. The resistor common to two meshes carries the difference of the two mesh currents, which gives the cross term with a minus sign. Solve the pair of simultaneous equations by substitution or determinants. |
| 8 | Choosing the Method | • Count nodes and meshes first.<br>• Fewer equations means less work.<br>• Nodal suits current sources.<br>• Mesh suits voltage sources.<br>• Both give the same answer.<br>**Formula/Key:** Equations: nodal n − 1;   mesh b − n + 1 (b branches, n nodes) | Comparison table of nodal and mesh analysis. | The two methods are equivalent and give identical results, so choose the one needing fewer equations. If there are fewer nodes than meshes, use nodal analysis. If voltage sources dominate, mesh analysis is usually simpler. Take care when the network contains the other kind of source, since this adds an extra constraint. |
| 9 | Power and Energy | • Power: rate of energy conversion.<br>• Resistor power turns into heat.<br>• Energy = power × time.<br>• Utility unit: kWh (one unit).<br>**Formula/Key:** P = VI = I²R = V²/R   (W);   W = P·t   (J, or kWh)<br>1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J | Four cards with the power formulas and the energy relation. | Power in watts is the product of voltage and current. Using Ohm's law it can be written as I squared R or V squared over R, whichever quantities are known. Energy is power multiplied by time, in joules when the time is in seconds and in kilowatt hours for billing. The electricity meter at home counts kilowatt hours, also called units. |
| 10 | Sign Convention for Power | • Current into the + terminal: element absorbs.<br>• Current out of the + terminal: element delivers.<br>• Resistors always absorb.<br>• Total power delivered = total power absorbed.<br>**Formula/Key:** Conservation: ΣP(delivered) = ΣP(absorbed) | Two element symbols showing absorbing and delivering conventions with current arrows. | To decide whether an element supplies or consumes power, look at the direction of current relative to the polarity. If the current enters the positive terminal, the element absorbs power. A battery that is charging absorbs power, while the same battery discharging delivers power. Check your answer by verifying that delivered and absorbed powers are equal. |
| 11 | Solved Example 1: Nodal and Mesh | • V1 = 10 V, V2 = 2 V, R1 = 1 Ω, R2 = 2 Ω, R3 = 3 Ω (as drawn). Find Va, branch currents, power in each resistor.<br>• 1.
