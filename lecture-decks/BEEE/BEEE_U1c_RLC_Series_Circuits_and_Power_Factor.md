| 4 | Motivation: Why Power Factor? | • Motors and tube lights are inductive loads.<br>• Reactive current heats lines but does no work.<br>• Utilities penalise low power factor.<br>• Capacitors in the panel correct it.<br>**Formula/Key:** For the same real power, lower pf means more current and bigger losses | Bar chart of line current for 1 kW at 230 V at pf = 1, 0.8 and 0.5. | A factory with many induction motors draws more current than needed for its useful power. That extra current is reactive current. It increases cable loss and may force the supply company to install bigger equipment, so low power factor attracts a penalty on the electricity bill. To understand why, we need the phase relationships of R, L and C. |
| 5 | Pure Resistor | • v = Vm sin ωt gives i = (Vm/R) sin ωt.<br>• Voltage and current are in phase.<br>• Impedance equals R, independent of frequency.<br>• Average power P = VI = I²R.<br>**Formula/Key:** V = I·R,   φ = 0°,   P = V·I = I²R | Phasors V and I along the same direction; v and i waves coincide. | For a resistor the instantaneous current is simply the voltage divided by R. They rise and fall together, so the phase angle is zero. The power is always positive and is dissipated as heat. A resistor is the only one of the three elements that consumes real power on average. |
| 6 | Pure Inductor | • v = L·di/dt, so current lags voltage.<br>• Current lags by 90°.<br>• Inductive reactance XL = ωL = 2πfL.<br>• Average power is zero; energy is stored and returned.<br>**Formula/Key:** XL = 2πfL (Ω);   I = V/XL;   φ = −90°<br>L henry, f hertz | Phasor diagram with I 90° behind V and the corresponding waves. | The voltage across an inductor is L times the rate of change of current. When the current is increasing fastest the voltage is highest, so the current peak comes a quarter cycle later: the current lags by ninety degrees. Reactance grows with frequency, so an inductor passes low frequencies easily. It stores energy in the magnetic field and returns it, so the average power is zero. |
| 7 | Pure Capacitor | • i = C·dv/dt, so current leads voltage.<br>• Current leads by 90°.<br>• Capacitive reactance XC = 1/(2πfC).<br>• Average power is zero.<br>**Formula/Key:** XC = 1/(2πfC) (Ω);   I = V/XC;   φ = +90°<br>C farad, f hertz | Phasor diagram with I 90° ahead of V and the corresponding waves. | For a capacitor the current is proportional to the rate of change of voltage, so the current peaks a quarter cycle before the voltage. Capacitive reactance falls as frequency rises, the opposite of the inductor. Like an inductor, an ideal capacitor stores and returns energy, so no real power is consumed. A useful memory aid is CIVIL: in a capacitor I leads V, in an inductor V leads I. |
| 8 | Series RL Circuit | • Same current flows through R and L.<br>• VR in phase with I; VL leads I by 90°.<br>• Impedance Z = R + jXL.<br>• Current lags the supply voltage by φ.<br>**Formula/Key:** ∣Z∣ = √(R² + XL²);   φ = tan⁻¹(XL/R);   I = V/∣Z∣<br>V² = VR² + VL² | Left: series R–L circuit; right: impedance triangle with R, XL, Z and φ. | Take the current as the reference phasor. The resistor voltage is in phase with it and the inductor voltage leads it by ninety degrees, so the supply voltage is their phasor sum. Dividing by the current gives the impedance triangle with R on the base and X L as the height. The phase angle is the arctangent of X L over R, and the current lags the supply. |
| 9 | Series RC Circuit | • VR in phase with I; VC lags I by 90°.<br>• Impedance Z = R − jXC.<br>• Current leads the supply voltage by φ.<br>• Used in lag compensators and filters.<br>**Formula/Key:** ∣Z∣ = √(R² + XC²);   φ = tan⁻¹(XC/R);   I = V/∣Z∣ | Left: series R–C circuit; right: impedance triangle drawn downward for the negative reactance. | The capacitor voltage lags the current by ninety degrees, so the reactance is drawn downward in the impedance triangle. The total current leads the supply voltage by the angle phi. Series RC networks are common in filters and timing circuits. You will see the same RC network again when we meet the 555 timer. |
| 10 | Series RLC Circuit | • Z = R + j(XL − XC).<br>• XL > XC: inductive, current lags.<br>• XL < XC: capacitive, current leads.<br>• XL = XC: resonance, Z = R, I maximum.<br>**Formula/Key:** ∣Z∣ = √[R² + (XL − XC)²];   φ = tan⁻¹[(XL − XC)/R];   f₀ = 1/(2π√LC) | Series R–L–C circuit and three phasor diagrams: inductive, resonant, capacitive. | In the RLC circuit the inductor and capacitor voltages are opposite in direction, so their reactances subtract. If the inductive reactance is larger the circuit behaves like an RL circuit, otherwise like an RC circuit. When the two are equal the reactive parts cancel, the impedance is just R and the current is maximum. That is series resonance at f naught equal to one over two pi root L C. |
| 11 | Real, Reactive and Apparent Power | • Real power P is dissipated in R.<br>• Reactive power Q is exchanged with L and C.<br>• Apparent power S = V·I is the rating of equipment.<br>• Power triangle: P, Q, S.<br>**Formula/Key:** P = VI cos φ = I²R (W);   Q = VI sin φ = I²X (var);   S = VI (VA)<br>S² = P² + Q² | Power triangle with P on the base, Q as the height and S as the hypotenuse; φ at the left. | Multiplying the impedance triangle by the square of the current gives the power triangle. The base is the real power in watts that does useful work. The vertical side is the reactive power in var that merely oscillates between the source and the field. The hypotenuse is the apparent power in volt-amperes, which determines the size of the cables and transformers. |
| 12 | Power Factor | • pf = cos φ = P/S.<br>• Lagging for RL loads, leading for RC loads.<br>• Low pf: more current for the same P.<br>• Correction: add a capacitor in parallel.<br>• Utility tariffs reward pf near 1.<br>**Formula/Key:** pf = cos φ = R/∣Z∣;   I = P/(V·pf) | Bar chart showing line current for the same 1 kW at three power factors. | Power factor is the cosine of the phase angle, or the ratio of real to apparent power. For the same real power the current is inversely proportional to the power factor, so half the power factor doubles the current and quadruples the I squared R loss in the lines. A capacitor connected across the inductive load supplies the reactive power locally, which raises the power factor. |
| 13 | Solved Example 1: Series RL | • R = 30 Ω in series with L = 0.1273 H on 230 V, 50 Hz. Find Z, I, φ, pf, P, Q, S.<br>• 1. XL = 2π·50·0.1273 = 40 Ω.<br>• 2. Z = 30 + j40 → ∣Z∣ = 50 Ω.<br>• 3. I = 230/50 = 4.6 A;  φ = tan⁻¹(40/30) = 53.1°.<br>• 4. pf = cos 53.1° = 0.6 lagging.<br>• 5. P = I²R = 634.8 W;  Q = I²XL = 846.4 var.<br>• 6. S = VI = 1058 VA.<br>**Formula/Key:** Answer: I = 4.6 A lagging by 53.1°, pf = 0.6, P = 635 W, Q = 846 var, S = 1058 VA | Left: steps; right: impedance triangle and power triangle with numbers. | Compute the reactance first and then the impedance magnitude using the three-four-five triangle with a multiplier of ten. The current is the supply voltage over the impedance. Power factor is R over Z, 0.6. Compute P, Q and S, and verify that S squared equals P squared plus Q squared: about 1058 squared against 635 squared plus 846 squared. |
