| 5 | The p-n Junction | • Holes and electrons diffuse across the junction.<br>• They recombine, leaving fixed ions.<br>• Depletion region forms; a built-in field appears.<br>• Barrier potential opposes further diffusion.<br>• Si ≈ 0.7 V; Ge ≈ 0.3 V.<br>**Formula/Key:** Barrier potential: Si ≈ 0.7 V, Ge ≈ 0.3 V at room temperature | p-region, n-region and the central depletion region with fixed ions and the built-in field. | When the p and n materials are joined, holes diffuse into the n side and electrons into the p side. They recombine, leaving a region with no mobile carriers, called the depletion region. The fixed ions create an electric field that opposes further diffusion. The potential across it is the barrier potential, about 0.7 volts in silicon. |
| 6 | Forward and Reverse Bias | • Forward: p to +, n to − of the source.<br>• Depletion narrows; current flows above 0.7 V.<br>• Reverse: p to −, n to +.<br>• Depletion widens; only a tiny leakage flows.<br>• Resistor in series limits forward current.<br>**Formula/Key:** Forward: conducts when V > Vγ;   reverse: I ≈ Is (nA–µA) until breakdown | Two circuits: forward-biased diode with a resistor and a reverse-biased diode. | In forward bias the external voltage opposes the barrier, the depletion region shrinks and a large current flows once the voltage exceeds about 0.7 volts. In reverse bias the external voltage adds to the barrier, so the region widens and only a very small leakage current of minority carriers flows. Always use a series resistor with a forward-biased diode to limit the current. |
| 7 | Diode V–I Characteristic | • Forward: exponential rise after the knee.<br>• Knee voltage Vγ ≈ 0.7 V (Si).<br>• Reverse: small, nearly constant leakage.<br>• Breakdown at high reverse voltage.<br>• Leakage doubles about every 10 °C.<br>**Formula/Key:** I = Is·(e^(V/(n·VT)) − 1);   VT = kT/q ≈ 26 mV at 300 K<br>Is reverse saturation current, n ideality factor (1–2) | Diode I–V curve: knee at 0.7 V forward, leakage and breakdown in reverse (schematic). | The Shockley equation describes the characteristic. Below the knee, about 0.5 volts for silicon, the current is negligible. Above the knee the current rises very rapidly, so the voltage across a conducting diode stays near 0.7 volts. In reverse the current is a tiny saturation current, until the breakdown voltage is reached. Temperature affects both the knee and the leakage. |
| 8 | Diode Equivalent Circuits | • Ideal: a switch, no drop.<br>• Practical: ideal diode plus 0.7 V battery.<br>• Piecewise linear: add a small resistance rd.<br>• Choose the simplest model that is accurate enough.<br>• Reverse: open circuit in all models.<br>**Formula/Key:** Ideal: V = 0 if on;   Practical: V = Vγ;   Linear: V = Vγ + I·rd | Three rows: ideal, ideal + Vγ, ideal + Vγ + rd, each with its equivalent circuit and I–V line. | Solving with the exponential equation is difficult, so we replace the diode by a simple equivalent circuit. The ideal model is a perfect switch. The practical model adds a 0.7 volt battery to represent the knee. The third model adds a small resistance for the slope of the characteristic. For most problems in this course the practical model is enough. |
| 9 | Analysing a Diode Circuit | • Decide: forward or reverse biased?<br>• Replace the diode by its equivalent circuit.<br>• Apply KVL around the loop.<br>• Solve for the current.<br>• Check the assumption.<br>**Formula/Key:** Series diode–resistor:  I = (V − Vγ)/R   (V > Vγ);   I ≈ 0 if reverse biased | Series circuit with 10 V source, 1 kΩ resistor and a silicon diode; I computed below. | First guess the state of the diode. If the source drives current from anode to cathode with more than 0.7 volts, assume it conducts. Replace it by a 0.7 volt drop, write Kirchhoff's voltage law and find the current. Then check: the current must be positive, otherwise the diode is off and the current is zero. Here the current is 9.3 milliamperes. |
| 10 | Zener Diode | • Heavily doped diode designed to operate in breakdown.<br>• Reverse breakdown voltage Vz is sharp and stable.<br>• Voltage stays near Vz over a wide current range.<br>• Safe current limited by power rating.<br>• Symbol: diode with bent bar.<br>**Formula/Key:** Operation: reverse biased, Iz,min < Iz < Iz,max;   Pz = Vz·Iz ≤ Pz,max | Reverse characteristic with vertical breakdown at −Vz and the safe current limits marked. | A Zener diode is made for operation in the reverse breakdown region. In breakdown the voltage across it stays almost constant at Vz while the current may change widely. This makes it ideal as a reference or a simple voltage regulator. The current must stay above a minimum to remain in breakdown and below a maximum set by the power rating. |
| 11 | Zener Voltage Regulator | • Series resistor Rs, Zener in parallel with load.<br>• Output Vo = Vz while the Zener is in breakdown.<br>• Source current splits: Iz and IL.<br>• Iz absorbs changes in Vin or load.<br>• Need Iz,min ≤ Iz ≤ Iz,max.<br>**Formula/Key:** Is = (Vin − Vz)/Rs;   IL = Vz/RL;   Iz = Is − IL | Circuit with Vin, Rs, shunt Zener Dz, load RL and the three currents marked. | The Zener is connected in parallel with the load, in reverse, with a series resistor from the supply. The output voltage is held at Vz. The current through Rs is fixed by the difference between input and Zener voltage. That current divides between the load and the Zener, so if the load current changes, the Zener current changes by the opposite amount and the output stays constant. |
| 12 | Regulator Behaviour and Limits | • Line regulation: Vin changes, Vo steady.<br>• Load regulation: RL changes, Vo steady.<br>• Regulation fails when Iz < Iz,min.<br>• No-load case: Iz = Is must be ≤ Iz,max.<br>• Choose Rs to meet both limits.<br>**Formula/Key:** Rs,max = (Vin,min − Vz)/(Iz,min + IL,max);   Rs,min = (Vin,max − Vz)/(Iz,max + IL,min) | Four cards: Vin rises, Vin falls, RL falls, RL open, with the effect on Iz. | As the input voltage rises, the Zener takes more current and the output stays at Vz. As the load draws more current, the Zener current falls. The limit comes when the Zener current drops to its minimum, since then it leaves breakdown and regulation is lost. At no load all the series current flows through the Zener, which must not exceed its power rating. The choice of Rs balances these two limits. |

If the diode is reversed it blocks, no current flows and the whole supply voltage appears across the diode. The comparison shows when the ideal model is accurate enough. |
| 14 | Solved Example 2: Zener Regulator | • Vin = 20 V, Rs = 200 Ω, Vz = 10 V. Find Is, IL, Iz for RL = 1 kΩ and for RL = 250 Ω. Find RL,min if Iz,min = 5 mA.<br>• 1. Is = (20 − 10)/200 = 50 mA (both cases).<br>• 2. RL = 1 kΩ: IL = 10 mA, Iz = 40 mA.<br>• 3. RL = 250 Ω: IL = 40 mA, Iz = 10 mA.<br>• 4. With a light load the Zener carries most of it. With a heavier load the load current rises and the Zener current drops. The minimum load resistance is found by letting the Zener keep only its minimum current: all the remainder goes to the load. |
