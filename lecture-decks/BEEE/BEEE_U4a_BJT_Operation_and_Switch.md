| 4 | Motivation: One Device, Two Jobs | • Amplifier: small signal controls big current.<br>• Switch: turns lamps, relays and motors on and off.<br>• Processors use millions of switches.<br>• Interface between logic and power.<br>**Formula/Key:** A small base current controls a much larger collector current | Transistor driving a lamp: OFF state and ON state boxes (preview of the switch slide). | A transistor can act as an amplifier or as a switch. | 5 | BJT Construction | • Three layers: emitter, base, collector.<br>• npn: n–p–n sandwich; pnp: p–n–p.<br>• Emitter: heavily doped, emits carriers.<br>• Base: very thin, lightly doped.<br>• Collector: largest, collects carriers.<br>**Formula/Key:** Arrow on the emitter shows conventional current direction (out for npn, in for pnp) | npn layer diagram with doping notes and the circuit symbols. | The bipolar junction transistor has three regions. The emitter is heavily doped so it can inject many carriers. The base is very thin and lightly doped so that almost all of those carriers get through it. The collector is physically the largest because it must dissipate heat. The arrow on the emitter of the symbol shows the direction of conventional current. |
| 6 | Operation of an npn Transistor | • Active mode: EB forward, CB reverse.<br>• Emitter injects electrons into the base.<br>• Base is thin: only about 1 % recombine → IB.<br>• Reverse CB field sweeps the rest to the collector.<br>• Small IB controls large IC.<br>**Formula/Key:** IE = IB + IC;   IC ≈ IE (α ≈ 0.99) | Layer diagram with electron flow from emitter to collector and the small base current. | With the emitter-base junction forward biased and the collector-base junction reverse biased, electrons flow from the emitter into the base. Since the base is very thin, only a few recombine with holes and form the base current. The reverse-biased collector junction pulls the other electrons across into the collector, forming the collector current. A tiny change in the base current changes the collector current by a factor beta. |
| 7 | Current Relations | • β (hFE): current gain, typically 50–300.<br>• α: fraction of IE reaching the collector.<br>• IC = β·IB in the active region.<br>• Emitter current is the largest.<br>**Formula/Key:** IC = β·IB;   IE = IB + IC = (β + 1)·IB;   α = β/(β + 1)<br>β dimensionless, currents in A or mA | Transistor symbol with IB, IC, IE arrows and the three relations in cards. | Beta is the ratio of collector to base current. It varies from one transistor to another and with temperature, so design should not depend on its exact value. The emitter current is the sum of the base and collector currents, so it is slightly larger than the collector current. Alpha, the ratio IC over IE, is close to one. |
| 8 | Common-Emitter Output Characteristics | • IC against VCE for different base currents.<br>• Active region: curves nearly flat, IC ≈ β·IB.<br>• Saturation: small VCE, IC rises steeply.<br>• Cut-off: IB = 0, IC ≈ 0.<br>• Spacing of curves reflects β.<br>**Formula/Key:** Active: IC ≈ β·IB;   saturation: VCE(sat) ≈ 0.2 V;   cut-off: IC ≈ ICEO (tiny) | Family of IC–VCE curves for IB = 20, 40, 60, 80 µA with saturation, active and cut-off marked. | In the common-emitter configuration the base is the input and the collector the output. Each curve is for a fixed base current. For VCE above a few tenths of a volt the curve is nearly flat, so the collector current is set by the base current. At very low VCE the transistor is saturated and the current drops. When the base current is zero only a negligible leakage current flows. |
| 9 | Regions of Operation | • Cut-off: both junctions reverse biased.<br>• Active: EB forward, CB reverse.<br>• Saturation: both junctions forward biased.<br>• Amplifier works in the active region.<br>• Switch works between cut-off and saturation.<br>**Formula/Key:** Cut-off: IB = 0, switch OFF;   Saturation: IC = IC(sat), switch ON | Three-row table: region, EB junction, CB junction, use. | The state of the two junctions defines the operating region. In cut-off both are reverse biased, so no current flows and the switch is open. In the active region the emitter junction is forward and the collector junction reverse biased, the condition for amplification. In saturation both are forward biased, the collector voltage is only a fraction of a volt, and the switch is closed. |
| 10 | DC Load Line | • Collector circuit: VCC = IC·RC + VCE.<br>• Straight line on the output characteristics.<br>• End points: (VCE = VCC, IC = 0) and (0, VCC/RC).<br>• Operating (Q) point lies on the load line.<br>• Q is set by the base current.<br>**Formula/Key:** IC = (VCC − VCE)/RC;   example VCC = 12 V, RC = 2 kΩ: (12 V, 0) and (0, 6 mA)<br>Q: IB = 40 µA → IC = 4 mA, VCE = 4 V | Output characteristics with the DC load line and the Q-point at (4 V, 4 mA) marked. | Writing Kirchhoff's voltage law round the collector circuit gives a straight line relation between IC and VCE. Its end points are the cut-off point, where VCE equals VCC, and the saturation point, where IC equals VCC over RC. The transistor must operate on this line, at the point where it crosses the curve of the chosen base current. This is the Q-point and it fixes the DC bias. |
| 11 | BJT as a Switch | • Input low: cut-off, transistor OFF.<br>• Input high: saturation, transistor ON.<br>• Load gets full VCC when ON, nothing when OFF.<br>• VCE(sat) ≈ 0.2 V: nearly an ideal closed switch.<br>• Used for LEDs, relays, motors and logic gates.<br>**Formula/Key:** OFF: IC ≈ 0, VCE ≈ VCC;   ON: IC = (VCC − VCE(sat))/RC, VCE ≈ 0.2 V | NPN switch driving a lamp from VCC, with OFF and ON state boxes. | A transistor switch is operated at the two ends of the load line. With zero input voltage the base current is zero and the transistor is cut off: the collector is at VCC and the lamp is off. With a high input voltage a large base current drives the transistor into saturation, the collector voltage falls to about 0.2 volts and the lamp gets nearly the whole supply. The device acts as a fast electronic switch with no moving parts. |
| 12 | Designing a BJT Switch | • Find IC(sat) from the load.<br>• Required IB(min) = IC(sat)/βmin.<br>• Overdrive: use 2–5 × IB(min).<br>• Calculate RB from the input voltage.<br>• Choose the nearest standard value.<br>**Formula/Key:** IC(sat) = (VCC − VCE(sat))/RC;   IB ≥ IC(sat)/β;   RB = (Vin − VBE)/IB | Switch circuit with RB, RC and the four design formulas in boxes beside it. | Start from the load: the collector current in saturation is set by the supply and the load resistor. The base current must be large enough to keep the transistor saturated even for the lowest possible beta, so divide by beta and multiply by an overdrive factor. Then compute the base resistor from the input voltage minus the 0.7 volt base-emitter drop. |
| 13 | Solved Example 1: BJT Currents | • A transistor with β = 100 has IB = 40 µA in the active region. Find IC, IE and α.<br>• 1. IC = β·IB = 100 × 40 µA = 4 mA.<br>• 2. IE = IB + IC = 0.04 + 4 = 4.04 mA.<br>• 3. α = IC/IE = 4/4.04 = 0.990.<br>• 4. Check: α = β/(β + 1) = 100/101 = 0.990.<br>**Formula/Key:** Answer: IC = 4 mA, IE = 4.04 mA, α = 0.990 | Left: steps; right: chain of four cards from IB to IC, IE and α. | The collector current is beta times the base current. The emitter current is the sum of base and collector currents, which is a little more than the collector current. The ratio of IC to IE is alpha, which agrees with beta over beta plus one. Always keep units consistent: microamperes and milliamperes. |
| 14 | Solved Example 2: Switch Design | • Drive a 200 Ω load from 12 V using a 5 V logic signal. βmin = 100, VBE = 0.7 V, VCE(sat) = 0.2 V. Find RB.<br>• 1. IC(sat) = (12 − 0.2)/200 = 59 mA.<br>• 2. IB(min) = 59 mA/100 = 0.59 mA.<br>• 3. Overdrive × 2: IB = 1.18 mA.<br>• 4. RB = (5 − 0.7)/1.18 mA = 3.64 kΩ.<br>• 5. Divide by the minimum beta to get the minimum base current, then double it to be safe. The base resistor follows from Ohm's law with the 0.7 volt drop. Picking the next lower standard value, 3.3 kilo-ohms, gives slightly more base current, which keeps the transistor firmly in saturation. |
