| 4 | Motivation: AC Mains to DC Supply | • Electronics need steady DC.<br>• Mains is 50 Hz AC.<br>• Chargers and adapters contain rectifiers.<br>• Rectifier is the key step.<br>**Formula/Key:** AC → transformer → rectifier → filter → regulator → DC load | Block chain of a DC power supply with a mini waveform under each block. | Every mobile charger or radio adapter converts AC to DC. | 5 | Half-Wave Rectifier | • One diode in series with the load.<br>• Positive half-cycle: diode conducts.<br>• Negative half-cycle: diode blocks.<br>• Output is a series of positive half-sine pulses.<br>• Output has a DC component plus ripple.<br>**Formula/Key:** vo = Vm sin ωt for 0 ≤ ωt ≤ π;   vo = 0 for π ≤ ωt ≤ 2π | Circuit with source, diode and load, and the waveforms of input (dashed) and output. | In the positive half-cycle the diode is forward biased and the load voltage follows the input. In the negative half-cycle the diode is reverse biased and no current flows. So only the positive half-cycles appear across the load. The output is unidirectional, but it is far from steady DC because it is zero for half the time. |
| 6 | Half-Wave Rectifier: Performance | • Average value: Vdc = Vm/π.<br>• RMS value: Vrms = Vm/2.<br>• Ripple factor 1.21: very poor.<br>• Efficiency only 40.6 %.<br>• Diode must withstand Vm in reverse (PIV).<br>**Formula/Key:** Vdc = Vm/π;  Idc = Im/π;  Vrms = Vm/2;  r = √[(Vrms/Vdc)² − 1] = 1.21;  PIV = Vm<br>η = 0.406 (maximum) | Four cards with Vdc, Vrms, ripple factor and efficiency, PIV. | The average value of the half-wave output is Vm over pi and the RMS value is Vm over two. The ripple factor is the ratio of the AC to the DC content, which is 1.21: the ripple is bigger than the DC itself. The efficiency is at most 40.6 per cent. The diode must be rated to withstand the peak inverse voltage, which is the full peak of the input. |
| 7 | Full-Wave Bridge Rectifier | • Four diodes in a bridge.<br>• Positive half-cycle: D1 and D4 conduct.<br>• Negative half-cycle: D2 and D3 conduct.<br>• Current through the load has the same direction.<br>• No centre-tapped transformer needed.<br>**Formula/Key:** vo = ∣Vm sin ωt∣ (minus two diode drops in practice) | Bridge circuit with four diodes, AC source on the left and load on the right; conducting pairs listed. | In the bridge circuit each half-cycle uses a different pair of diodes, but in both the current flows through the load in the same direction. The top terminal of the bridge is always positive. In practice two diodes are in series with the load, so the peak output is Vm minus 1.4 volts. A bridge needs only a simple transformer without a centre tap. |
| 8 | Bridge Rectifier: Performance | • Both half-cycles are used.<br>• Vdc doubles: 2Vm/π.<br>• Ripple frequency doubles to 2f.<br>• Efficiency 81.2 %.<br>• PIV per diode is Vm.<br>**Formula/Key:** Vdc = 2Vm/π = 0.636Vm;  Vrms = Vm/√2;  r = 0.482;  η = 81.2 %;  PIV = Vm | Full-wave rectified waveform with the average level Vdc marked. | Because both half-cycles reach the load, the average value doubles compared with the half-wave. The ripple factor falls to 0.48 and the efficiency doubles to 81 per cent. The ripple frequency is twice the supply frequency, which makes filtering easier. Each diode only needs to block Vm when it is off. |
| 9 | Half-Wave vs Bridge | • Bridge: higher Vdc, lower ripple.<br>• Bridge: twice the efficiency.<br>• Half-wave: only one diode, simplest.<br>• Bridge diodes: lower PIV than centre-tap.<br>• Ripple frequency: f against 2f.<br>**Formula/Key:** Prefer the bridge for power supplies; half-wave for very small loads | Comparison table: diodes, Vdc, ripple factor, efficiency, ripple frequency, PIV. | Compare the two on the table. The bridge needs four diodes but gives a higher average voltage, a smaller ripple and twice the efficiency. The half-wave circuit is cheap and used only for very small loads such as indicator lamps. In both cases the diode must withstand a reverse voltage equal to Vm. |
| 10 | Capacitor Filter | • Capacitor charges to the peak.<br>• It discharges slowly through the load.<br>• Output becomes nearly steady.<br>• Larger C or RL: smaller ripple.<br>• Diodes conduct in short pulses.<br>**Formula/Key:** Vdc ≈ Vm − Vr/2;   r = 1/(4√3·f·RL·C) (full-wave);   r = 1/(2√3·f·RL·C) (half-wave) | Rectified waveform (dashed) with the capacitor output following the peaks and discharging between them. | A capacitor connected across the load charges to the peak of the rectified voltage and then supplies the load while the rectified voltage falls. The output only drops by the ripple voltage before the next peak recharges it. Bigger capacitance or a larger load resistance gives less ripple. The trade-off is a larger peak current in the diodes. |
| 11 | Solved Example 1: Half-Wave | • A half-wave rectifier has a transformer secondary of 18 V RMS, RL = 100 Ω, Si diode. Find Vm, Vdc, Idc, Vrms and PIV.<br>• 1. Vm = 18√2 = 25.46 V.<br>• 2. Load peak = 25.46 − 0.7 = 24.76 V.<br>• 3. Vdc = 24.76/π = 7.88 V;  Idc = 78.8 mA.<br>• 4. Vrms = 24.76/2 = 12.38 V.<br>• 5. PIV = Vm = 25.46 V.<br>**Formula/Key:** Answer: Vdc = 7.88 V, Idc = 78.8 mA, Vrms = 12.4 V, PIV = 25.5 V | Left: steps; right: input (dashed) and half-wave output with Vdc marked. | Convert the RMS secondary voltage to peak. Subtract the diode drop to get the load peak voltage. The average voltage is the load peak over pi and the current follows from Ohm's law. The RMS value of a half-wave is half of the peak. The diode must withstand the full secondary peak when reverse biased, so choose a diode with a PIV rating above 26 volts. |
| 12 | Solved Example 2: Bridge Rectifier | • A bridge rectifier is fed from 24 V RMS and has RL = 200 Ω, Si diodes. Find Vdc, Idc, PIV and the ripple factor with a 1000 µF capacitor.<br>• 1. The average output is twice the peak over pi. A smoothing capacitor raises the DC voltage towards the peak and reduces the ripple factor to about 1.4 per cent for this load.
