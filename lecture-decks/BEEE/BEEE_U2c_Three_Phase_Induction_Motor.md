| 3 | Recap: What You Already Know | • Three-phase supply: three voltages 120° apart.<br>• Faraday: moving field induces EMF.<br>• Lenz: induced current opposes the cause.<br>• Force on a current-carrying conductor.<br>**Formula/Key:** VL = √3·Vph (star);   phase sequence R–Y–B | Three sinusoids R, Y, B displaced by 120°. | The three-phase supply has three voltages of equal magnitude displaced by one hundred twenty degrees. Faraday's law tells us that a moving magnetic field induces an EMF in a conductor, and Lenz's law says that the resulting current opposes the cause. Together with the force on a current-carrying conductor, these are all we need to understand the induction motor. |
| 4 | Motivation: The Industrial Workhorse | • Over 70 % of industrial motors.<br>• Pumps, fans, compressors, conveyors.<br>• Rugged, cheap, little maintenance.<br>• Runs directly from the three-phase mains.<br>**Formula/Key:** Three-phase induction motor: no commutator, no brushes (squirrel cage) | Cross-section of a three-phase induction motor stator with the rotor in the middle. | Look around a factory and almost every motor is a three-phase induction motor. It has no commutator and, in the squirrel cage form, no brushes or slip rings, so it is rugged and needs little maintenance. It connects directly to the supply and starts by itself. To see why, we need to understand the rotating field. |
| 5 | Three-Phase Supply and Stator Winding | • Three coils placed 120° apart in the stator.<br>• Each coil carries one phase current.<br>• Currents are 120° apart in time.<br>• The three fluxes combine in space.<br>**Formula/Key:** φR = Φm sin ωt;   φY = Φm sin(ωt − 120°);   φB = Φm sin(ωt − 240°) | The three-phase waveforms with their 120° displacement marked. | The stator carries three identical windings placed one hundred twenty degrees apart in space. Each is connected to one phase, so their currents are one hundred twenty degrees apart in time. Each coil produces a pulsating flux along its own axis. The combination of the three pulsating fluxes is a flux of constant magnitude that rotates in space. |
| 6 | Rotating Magnetic Field | • Add the three fluxes at each instant.<br>• Resultant has constant magnitude 1.5Φm.<br>• Its direction advances 60° every 60° of ωt.<br>• It rotates once per supply cycle (2 poles).<br>• Reverse two phases to reverse direction.<br>**Formula/Key:** Φr = 1.5·Φm;   speed = synchronous speed Ns | Four snapshots at ωt = 0°, 60°, 120°, 180° with the three coil axes and the rotating resultant vector. | Take four instants a sixty degrees apart. At each one, add the three flux phasors along the coil axes. The resultant has the same length, one and a half times the peak flux of one phase, but its direction has turned by sixty degrees. So a magnetic field of constant size rotates around the stator. Swapping any two supply leads reverses the direction of rotation. |
| 7 | Synchronous Speed | • Speed of the rotating field is Ns.<br>• Depends on supply frequency and poles.<br>• More poles: lower speed.<br>• 50 Hz, 4 poles: 1500 rpm.<br>**Formula/Key:** Ns = 120·f / P  (rpm)<br>f Hz, P number of poles | Table of poles against synchronous speed at 50 Hz. | The field completes one revolution for each pair of poles per supply cycle. So the synchronous speed is 120 times the frequency divided by the number of poles. A two-pole stator on 50 hertz gives 3000 rpm and a four-pole stator gives 1500 rpm. The motor can never run faster than the field if it is to produce torque. |
| 8 | Principle of Operation | • Rotating field cuts the rotor conductors.<br>• EMF is induced (Faraday).<br>• Rotor circuit is closed: current flows.<br>• Current in the field gives force: torque.<br>• Rotor follows the field (Lenz) but lags it.<br>**Formula/Key:** Torque exists only if relative speed (Ns − N) ≠ 0 | Stator with rotating field at Ns and rotor rotating slightly slower at N; four numbered steps beside it. | The rotating field sweeps past the stationary rotor conductors, so EMFs are induced in them. Because the rotor circuit is closed, currents flow. These currents interact with the field and produce a torque in the direction of the field's rotation, as Lenz's law requires. The rotor accelerates but can never reach synchronous speed, because then there would be no relative motion and no induced EMF. |
| 9 | Slip | • Slip measures how far N lags Ns.<br>• Full-load slip is 2–5 %.<br>• At start N = 0, so s = 1.<br>• Rotor frequency is f·s: small at full speed.<br>**Formula/Key:** s = (Ns − N)/Ns;   N = Ns(1 − s);   fr = s·f | Speed axis from standstill (s = 1) to synchronous speed (s = 0) with the full-load slip region shaded. | Slip is the fractional difference between the field speed and the rotor speed. At standstill the slip is one, and at synchronous speed it is zero. At full load it is typically two to five per cent. The rotor currents have frequency s times f, which is only a few hertz at normal speed. That is why a rotor core can have small losses. |
| 10 | Constructional Features: Stator | • Frame: cast iron or steel, with cooling fins.<br>• Stator core: laminated silicon steel.<br>• Slots hold the three-phase winding.<br>• Winding terminals in a terminal box.<br>• Small air gap improves power factor.<br>**Formula/Key:** Stator winding: three coils displaced 120°; star or delta connected | Stator cross-section with coloured slot conductors for the three phases and the rotor inside. | The stator is a laminated core of silicon steel pressed into a cast frame. Slots in the inner surface carry the three-phase winding, whose ends are brought to a terminal box. The air gap between stator and rotor is kept very small, often less than a millimetre, so that the magnetising current and power factor are good. The motor can be connected in star or in delta. |
| 11 | Squirrel-Cage Rotor | • Laminated core with slots.<br>• Copper or aluminium bars in the slots.<br>• Bars joined by end rings: a short-circuited cage.<br>• No slip rings or brushes.<br>• Skewed bars reduce noise.<br>**Formula/Key:** Most common type: simple, rugged and cheap | Cage rotor: bars between two end rings, with shaft stubs. | The squirrel-cage rotor has solid bars of aluminium or copper in the slots, welded or cast to end rings at both ends. This forms a permanently short-circuited winding that looks like a cage. There are no brushes or slip rings, so it is robust and almost maintenance free. Its drawback is that its rotor resistance cannot be changed from outside, so the starting torque is moderate. |
| 12 | Wound-Rotor (Slip-Ring) Rotor | • Rotor carries a three-phase winding.<br>• Winding ends go to three slip rings.<br>• Brushes connect external resistance.<br>• High starting torque, low starting current.<br>• Speed control by rotor resistance.<br>**Formula/Key:** Rext cut out gradually as the motor accelerates | Rotor winding connected to three slip rings, brushes and an external resistance. | The wound rotor has an insulated three-phase winding, usually star connected, whose three ends are taken to slip rings on the shaft. Carbon brushes connect the rings to an external three-phase rheostat. A large external resistance at start gives a high torque at low starting current, and is cut out as the motor reaches speed. It is used in cranes and hoists, which need high starting torque. |
| 13 | Squirrel Cage vs Wound Rotor | • Cage: cheap, rugged, maintenance-free.<br>• Wound: slip rings, brushes, costlier.<br>• Wound rotor gives high starting torque.<br>• Wound rotor allows speed control.<br>• Stator is identical in both.<br>**Formula/Key:** Both run on the same rotating-field principle | Comparison table of the two rotor types. | The two motors differ only in the rotor. The squirrel-cage motor is cheaper and more reliable, so it is used in most applications. The wound-rotor motor costs more and needs maintenance of brushes and slip rings, but allows external resistance for high starting torque or limited speed control. Stator construction and principle of operation are the same. |
| 14 | Solved Example 1: Slip and Torque | • A 4-pole, 50 Hz induction motor runs at 1440 rpm and delivers 11 kW. Find Ns, slip, rotor frequency and shaft torque.<br>• 1. Ns = 120 × 50/4 = 1500 rpm.<br>• 2. s = (1500 − 1440)/1500 = 0.04 (4 %).<br>• 3. fr = s·f = 2 Hz.<br>• 4. ω = 2π × 1440/60 = 150.8 rad/s.<br>• 5. T = P/ω = 11000/150.8 = 72.9 N·m.<br>**Formula/Key:** Answer: Ns = 1500 rpm, slip 4 %, fr = 2 Hz, T = 72.9 N·m | Left: steps; right: chain of cards from Ns to slip, rotor frequency and torque. | Find the synchronous speed from frequency and poles, then the slip from the speed difference. The rotor frequency is slip times supply frequency, only two hertz here. Torque is output power divided by angular speed in radians per second. Remember to convert rpm to rad per second using two pi over sixty. |
| 15 | Solved Example 2: Speed from Slip | • A 6-pole, 50 Hz induction motor has full-load slip 4 %. Find the speed, and the speed if the load is reduced so that slip is 3 %.<br>• 1. Ns = 120 × 50/6 = 1000 rpm.<br>• 2. N = Ns(1 − s) = 1000 × 0.96 = 960 rpm.<br>• 3. At s = 3 %: N = 1000 × 0.97 = 970 rpm.<br>• 4. Rotor frequency at full load = 0.04 × 50 = 2 Hz.<br>**Formula/Key:** Answer: 960 rpm at full load, 970 rpm at lighter load; Ns = 1000 rpm | Left: steps; right: bar chart of Ns, N at 4 % slip and N at 3 % slip. | Compute the synchronous speed first. The rotor speed follows from N equals Ns times one minus s. Lighter load reduces the slip, so the speed rises a little but never reaches 1000 rpm. This shows why the induction motor is a nearly constant-speed machine. |
